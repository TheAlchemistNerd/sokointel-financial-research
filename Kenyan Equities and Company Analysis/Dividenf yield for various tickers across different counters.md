**1. Dividend Payout Ratio vs. Cash Flow Strength**

Evaluating how safely a company pays dividends requires two distinct
lenses: accounting profits and actual cash flows.
\[[[1]{.underline}](https://mail.reviewedjournals.com/index.php/Business/article/view/27),
[[2]{.underline}](https://www.cfainstitute.org/insights/professional-learning/refresher-readings/2026/analysis-of-dividends-and-share-repurchases),
[[3]{.underline}](https://www.arabianjbmr.com/pdfs/JPDS_VOL_9_1/12.pdf),
[[4]{.underline}](https://ca.finance.yahoo.com/news/6-yield-pays-every-30-200000844.html),
[[5]{.underline}](https://kenyanwallstreet.com/what-is-a-good-dividend-payout-ratio)\]

-   **Dividend Payout Ratio (DPR):** This shows what percentage of
    accounting net profit is distributed to shareholders.\
    \\(\\text{DPR}=\\frac{\\text{Dividends\\ Per\\ Share\\
    (DPS)}}{\\text{Earnings\\ Per\\ Share\\ (EPS)}}\\)\
    A ratio below 60% is generally healthy. Ratios approaching or
    exceeding 100% indicate the company is paying out more than it
    earns, risking a dividend cut.
    \[[[1]{.underline}](https://www.mycapitally.com/blog/how-to-evaluate-dividend-stocks),
    [[2]{.underline}](https://www.tasivalue.com/glossary),
    [[3]{.underline}](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/calculate-shareholder-yields-owners-earnings-david-j-waldron-cn5ne),
    [[4]{.underline}](https://kenyanwallstreet.com/what-is-a-good-dividend-payout-ratio)\]

-   **Free Cash Flow (FCF) Strength:** Net profits include non-cash
    items (like depreciation or unpaid invoices). Free Cash Flow
    represents actual cash left over after maintaining operations and
    paying for capital expenditures (CapEx).\
    \\(\\text{FCF}=\\text{Operating\\ Cash\\ Flow}-\\text{Capital\\
    Expenditures}\\)
    \[[[1]{.underline}](https://www.investopedia.com/terms/f/freecashflowpershare.asp),
    [[2]{.underline}](https://www.truedata.in/blog/free-cash-flow-ratios-explained),
    [[3]{.underline}](https://www.chaserhq.com/blog/cash-flow-definition-importance-and-examples),
    [[4]{.underline}](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MRXDVEDT-ac),
    [[5]{.underline}](https://www.mycapitally.com/blog/how-to-evaluate-dividend-stocks)\]

-   **The Comparison Test:** Check the **Dividend-to-FCF Ratio**
    (\\(\\frac{\\text{Total\\ Dividends\\ Paid}}{\\text{Free\\ Cash\\
    Flow}}\\)). If a company has a low accounting DPR but a very high
    Dividend-to-FCF ratio, its cash is trapped in working capital or
    heavy CapEx, rendering the high dividend yield unsustainable.
    \[[[1]{.underline}](https://dcf-model.com/blogs/blog/basics-payout-ratios),
    [[2]{.underline}](https://www.financestrategists.com/wealth-management/financial-statements/payout-ratio/),
    [[3]{.underline}](https://insightsoftware.com/encyclopedia/dividend-yield-analysis/),
    [[4]{.underline}](https://kenyanwallstreet.com/what-is-a-good-dividend-payout-ratio),
    [[5]{.underline}](https://www.mycapitally.com/blog/how-to-evaluate-dividend-stocks)\]

**2. Step-by-Step: How to Calculate Net Post-Tax Yields**

Here is the exact math to compare a T-Bill vs. a Dividend Stock so you
can decide where to place your next KSh 100,000.

**A. Calculating T-Bill Net Returns (The Discount Method)**

T-Bills are sold at a \"discount\" (less than face value) and redeem at
face value.

-   **Scenario:** You want to invest KSh 100,000 (Face Value) in a
    364-Day T-Bill with a **9.04%** yield.

-   **Step 1: Determine Purchase Price**\
    \\(\\text{Price}=\\frac{100,000}{(1+9.04\\%)}=\\text{KSh\\
    91,709}\\)\
    *(You pay KSh 91,709 today to get KSh 100,000 in one year.)*

-   **Step 2: Calculate Gross Interest**\
    \\(100,000-91,709=\\text{KSh\\ 8,291}\\)

-   **Step 3: Deduct 15% Withholding Tax**\
    \\(8,291\\times 15\\%=\\text{KSh\\ 1,243.65\\ (Tax)}\\)\
    \\(8,291-1,243.65=\\textbf{KSh\\ 7,047.35\\ (Net\\ Profit)}\\)

-   **Step 4: Calculate Net Yield**\
    \\(\\frac{7,047.35}{91,709}=\\textbf{7.68\\%}\\)

**B. Calculating Dividend Stock Net Returns**

-   **Scenario:** You invest KSh 91,709 (same amount) in **Standard
    Chartered (SCBK)** with a 9.3% dividend yield.

-   **Step 1: Calculate Gross Dividend**\
    \\(91,709\\times 9.3\\%=\\text{KSh\\ 8,529}\\)

-   **Step 2: Deduct 5% Withholding Tax**\
    \\(8,529\\times 5\\%=\\text{KSh\\ 426.45\\ (Tax)}\\)

-   **Step 3: Calculate Net Profit**\
    \\(8,529-426.45=\\textbf{KSh\\ 8,102.55\\ (Net\\ Profit)}\\)

-   **Step 4: Calculate Net Yield**\
    \\(\\frac{8,102.55}{91,709}=\\textbf{8.83\\%}\\)

**1. How to Compute Compounded Annual Growth Rate (CAGR)**

**CAGR represents the smoothed annual rate** at which your long-term
multi-asset portfolio grows, assuming all dividends are systematically
reinvested. Unlike a standard average, CAGR accounts for the compounding
effects of time.
\[[[1]{.underline}](https://www.kotak.bank.in/en/stories-in-focus/mutual-funds/what-is-cagr.html),
[[2]{.underline}](https://groww.in/p/cagr-compounded-annual-growth-rate),
[[3]{.underline}](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5uQH7VxdO64&t=39),
[[4]{.underline}](https://sharepredictions.com/tools/cagr-calculator/),
[[5]{.underline}](https://appreciatewealth.com/blog/what-is-cagr-in-mutual-fund)\]

**The Mathematical Formula**

\\(\\text{CAGR}=\\left(\\frac{\\text{Ending\\ Value}}{\\text{Beginning\\
Value}}\\right)\^{\\frac{1}{n}}-1\\)\
*(Where \\(n\\) = total number of years you held the investment)*.
\[[[1]{.underline}](https://www.kotak.bank.in/en/stories-in-focus/mutual-funds/what-is-cagr.html),
[[2]{.underline}](https://groww.in/p/cagr-compounded-annual-growth-rate)\]

**Step-by-Step Practical Scenario**

Assume you invested **KSh 600,000** into your multi-sector equity
basket. After **5 years** of market volatility, capital gains, and
dividend reinvestments, your portfolio wallet is valued at **KSh
1,020,000**.

-   **Step 1: Divide Ending Value by Beginning Value**\
    \\(\\frac{1,020,000}{600,000}=1.7\\)

-   **Step 2: Raise the result to the power of \\((1 \\div
    \\text{years})\\)**\
    \\(1.7\^{\\frac{1}{5}}=1.7\^{0.2}=1.1119\\)

-   **Step 3: Subtract 1 to convert to a percentage**\
    \\(1.1119-1=0.1119\\rightarrow \\mathbf{11.19\\%}\\)
    \[[[1]{.underline}](https://www.cmcmarkets.com/en-gb/fundamental-analysis/learn-cagr-explained),
    [[2]{.underline}](https://www.navexa.com/blog/how-to-calculate-compound-annual-growth-rate),
    [[3]{.underline}](https://www.incorpx.io/tools/cagr-calculator)\]

**The Metric\'s Value:** This means your blended investments grew by an
average of **11.19% every single year**, making it easy to compare

**1. The Post-Tax Yield Anomalies (The Core Puzzle)**

According to standard economic theory, high-risk assets (stocks) should
require a massive headline premium over risk-free assets (T-Bills) to
convince you to buy them. However, due to Kenya\'s tax architecture,
**the equity premium is artificially widened after taxes**, making the
puzzle even more glaring:
\[[[1]{.underline}](https://www.youtube.com/shorts/IzhNyWmY-m8),
[[2]{.underline}](https://groww.in/blog/rbis-operation-twist-all-you-need-to-know)\]

-   **The Risk-Free Baseline:** A 91-day T-Bill rollover gives you a
    gross yield of \~8.80% but drags a heavy **15% WHT**, pulling your
    guaranteed net yield down to **\~7.48%**.

-   **The Equity Income Baseline:** A high-yield stock like Standard
    Chartered (SCBK) or BAT pays a gross dividend yield of 9.3% to
    12.5%. Because it only attracts a **5% WHT**, its net yield sits
    between **8.83% and 11.87%**.

**The Puzzle:** You receive an immediate post-tax premium of **1.35% to
4.39% in cash alone** just by choosing equities over government debt.
From a purely rational cash-flow perspective, the premium is so high
that investors should theoretically abandon T-Bills entirely until
equity prices rise and yields compress. Yet, T-Bills remain heavily
oversubscribed.

**2. The Asymmetric Tax Distortion (CGT vs. Income Tax)**

The Equity Premium Puzzle deepens when you factor in long-term growth
tracking (CAGR).

-   **T-Bill Rollover Friction:** Every 91 days, the KRA clips 15% of
    your interest. You can only roll over the *remaining 85%* of your
    profits. This introduces heavy compounding friction over a
    5-to-10-year horizon.
    \[[[1]{.underline}](https://www.compoundwithrene.com/p/the-equity-yield-curve-why-the-long)\]

-   **The Equity Growth Loophole:** If you invest in growth counters
    like Safaricom (SCOM) or Equity Group (EQTY), your capital
    appreciation compounds at **100% tax-free velocity** because of the
    **0% CGT rule on the NSE**.

When you compute your 5-year CAGR, the stock portfolio does not just
beat the T-Bill rollover on paper; it completely outpaces it because
government debt interest is taxed instantly, while equity capital gains
are protected from the taxman entirely.

**3. Why the Puzzle Persists in Kenya (The Investor Psychology)**

If the math heavily favors equities due to low dividend taxes, zero CGT,
and high yields, why does the \"puzzle\" persist? Why hasn\'t a flood of
capital rushed into the NSE to erase this premium?

1.  **Extreme Loss Aversion:** Standard economic models assume investors
    look at long-term CAGR. In reality, local investors experience
    severe loss aversion. The fear of seeing a stock price drop by 20%
    in a market downturn outweighs the rational math of a 5% dividend
    tax advantage.

2.  **Liquidity Preferences:** The 91-day T-Bill rollover gives
    investors their principal back every 3 months with 100% certainty.
    An NSE stock portfolio---even a diversified multi-sector

**2. Corporate Earnings Volatility: The Hidden Risk Premium**

Standard academic models (like the Capital Asset Pricing Model or CAPM)
treat risk entirely as **price volatility** (Beta). They assume that if
a stock\'s price bounces around, investors simply demand a higher return
to cope with the stress of the choppy charts.
\[[[1]{.underline}](https://seekingalpha.com/article/3204646-the-great-beta-hoax-not-an-accurate-measure-of-risk-after-all)\]

However, standard models frequently fail because they ignore **Corporate
Earnings Volatility**, which behaves as a hidden, structural risk
premium in three distinct ways:

**A. The Illusion of the Flawless Dividend Yield**

When looking at an NSE stock table, a dividend yield is always
*trailing* (historical). Standard models look at a 10% dividend yield
and assume it operates like a fixed coupon. In reality, if economic
conditions deteriorate:

-   A government will always pay its T-Bill interest (even if it has to
    print money or issue new debt to do so).

-   A company board facing a cash crunch will instantly **slash or
    completely freeze its dividend** to protect its balance sheet.

**B. The Compounding Asymmetry**

If a bank earns KSh 10 per share in Year 1, KSh 2 in Year 2 (due to bad
loan provisions), and KSh 18 in Year 3, the *average* earnings look like
a stable KSh 10 per share.

However, because dividends are paid out of actual annual cash flows, the
company cannot smoothly distribute income. The sudden dividend freeze in
Year 2 breaks the multi-year compounding chain. A single year of zero
dividends destroys the geometric growth curve of your portfolio, a stark
contrast to the unbroken quarterly heartbeat of a 91-day T-Bill
rollover.

**C. Operating Leverage and the Bankruptcy Risk Floor**

Governments do not go out of business. Listed corporations, however,
carry fixed operational costs (salaries, factory leases, electricity). A
small 10% drop in a company\'s revenue can cause a catastrophic 50% drop
in net profit due to operating leverage.

This structural unpredictability means that the wide equity premium you
observe on the NSE is not a market irrationality---it is a mandatory
cushion. Investors require that massive post-tax premium to absorb the
unquantifiable risk that corporate earnings could evaporate during an
economic slowdown.

[[2]{.underline}](https://www.scribd.com/document/200956668/3185-ch04),

**2. CBK Capital Adequacy Limits: The Ultimate Dividend Kill-Switch**

The Central Bank of Kenya (CBK) prioritizes banking sector stability
over shareholder returns. If a bank's cash buffers fall below mandatory
statutory thresholds, the CBK invokes its legal powers to **completely
veto or block any dividend payouts**, regardless of how much accounting
profit the bank reported.

The CBK enforces three strict statutory capital adequacy ratios under
the Banking Act Prudential Guidelines:

\[CBK MANDATORY CAPITAL BUFFERS\]

Core Capital / Total Deposit Liabilities: ===\> Min 8.0%

Core Capital / Risk-Weighted Assets: =======\> Min 10.5% (Includes 2.5%
buffer)

Total Capital / Risk-Weighted Assets: ===========\> Min 14.5% (Includes
2.5% buffer)

**How the Kill-Switch Works in Practice:**

1.  **Risk-Weighting Assets:** A bank cannot treat all its assets
    equally. Cash and T-Bills carry a **0% risk weight** (completely
    safe). Loans to small businesses or unsecured personal loans carry a
    **100% risk weight** (high risk).

2.  **The Capital Multiplier:** As non-performing loans (NPLs) increase,
    the bank\'s \"Risk-Weighted Assets\" figure automatically inflates.

3.  **The Trigger:** If the bank\'s Core Capital to Risk-Weighted Assets
    drops even slightly below **10.5%** (or Total Capital drops below
    **14.5%**), the bank legally enters a capital deficit zone.

4.  **The Enforcement:** The CBK immediately restricts the bank\'s
    capital distribution privileges. Under these guidelines, the bank is
    legally prohibited from distributing cash to shareholders. All
    retained earnings must be redirected internally to rebuild the
    capital cushion until the ratio climbs back above the legal floor.

**1. Calculating the Dividend Payout Ratio for Manufacturing Stocks (BAT
& EABL)**

The **Dividend Payout Ratio (DPR)** measures the percentage of net
profit a manufacturing company distributes to its shareholders as cash
dividends rather than retaining it to build factories, buy raw
materials, or pay down corporate debt.
\[[[1]{.underline}](https://gocardless.com/guides/posts/how-to-calculate-the-dividend-payout-ratio),
[[2]{.underline}](https://www.vaia.com/en-us/textbooks/math/corporate-finance-9-edition/chapter-16/problem-8-homemade-leverage-star-inc-a-prominent-consumer-pr/),
[[3]{.underline}](https://groww.in/p/dividend-payout-ratio)\]

**The Formula:**

\\(\\text{Dividend\\ Payout\\ Ratio}=\\frac{\\text{Total\\ Dividends\\
Paid}}{\\text{Net\\ Profit\\ After\\ Tax}}\\quad \\text{or}\\quad
\\frac{\\text{Dividends\\ Per\\ Share\\ (DPS)}}{\\text{Earnings\\ Per\\
Share\\ (EPS)}}\\)

**Step-by-Step Practical Application**

Open the company\'s annual report or abridged full-year results PDF and
locate the **Statement of Comprehensive Income**:

1.  **Find Net Profit After Tax** (or look for *Earnings Per Share* in
    the footnotes).

2.  **Find Total Dividend Declared** (This is the sum of any *Interim
    Dividend* paid mid-year plus the proposed *Final Dividend*).
    \[[[1]{.underline}](https://about.tagnifi.com/which-shares-outstanding-should-i-use-in-my-valuation/),
    [[2]{.underline}](https://www.angelone.in/knowledge-center/share-market/dividend-payout-ratio),
    [[3]{.underline}](https://learning.uonbi.ac.ke/courses/DAC203/scormPackages/path_5/474_dividends.html)\]

**Case A: The Cash Cow Model (BAT Kenya BAT)**

-   **The Dynamics:** BAT operates a highly mature business model with
    low capital expenditure (CapEx) requirements. They already have
    established factories and highly stable regional export supply
    lines.

-   **The Math:** If BAT reports an EPS of KSh 40.00 and pays out a
    total DPS of KSh 35.00:\
    \\(\\text{DPR}=\\frac{35.00}{40.00}=\\mathbf{87.5\\%}\\)
    \[[[1]{.underline}](https://www.5paisa.com/stock-market-guide/stock-share-market/what-is-payout-ratio)\]

-   **Strategic Meaning:** A high DPR (\~80%+) means the company returns
    almost all its cash to you. Growth will be slow, but your immediate
    cash flow yield is maximized.

**Case B: The Growth/CapEx Model (East African Breweries EABL)**

-   **The Dynamics:** EABL operates in a highly competitive FMCG sector

-   **The Math:** If EABL reports an EPS of KSh 15.00 but distributes a

    \\(\\text{DPR}=\\frac{5.50}{15.00}=\\mathbf{36.6\\%}\\)

-   **Strategic Meaning:** A low DPR (\~30% to 50%) is not necessarily
    bad. It means the company keeps **63.4% of its cash** to fund
    expansions, lower its debt, and drive the future capital gains that
    boost your long-term portfolio CAGR.
    \[[[1]{.underline}](https://navi.com/blog/dividend-payout-ratio/)\]

For manufacturing giants like British American Tobacco (BAT) or East

The **Inventory Turnover Ratio** measures how many times a company sells

\[[[1]{.underline}](https://tallysolutions.com/inventory/inventory-turnover-ratio-meaning/),
[[2]{.underline}](https://www.tigernix.com/blog/inventory-turnover-ratio),

    [[2]{.underline}](https://businesslike.co.nz/inventory-turnover-ratio-calculation/),
    [[3]{.underline}](https://aico.ai/glossary/inventory-turnover-ratio)\]

2.  **Inventories (or Stock):** Found on the *Statement of Financial

    \[[[1]{.underline}](https://www.openriskmanagement.com/mathematical-formulation-of-financial-statements/),
    [[2]{.underline}](https://quizlet.com/vn/884467249/chap-3-as-flash-cards/),

\\(\\text{Inventory\\ Turnover\\ Ratio}=\\frac{\\text{Cost\\ of\\
Sales}}{\\text{Average\\ Inventory}}\\)

\\(\\text{Days\\ Sales\\ of\\ Inventory\\

\[[[1]{.underline}](https://www.inventory-planner.com/inventory-turnover-ratio/)\]**

**2. The Combined Ratio: Measuring Pure Underwriting Profitability**

For insurance counters like Jubilee Holdings, the **Combined Ratio** is
the ultimate metric of operational health. It measures whether an
insurance company is making a profit purely from the premiums it
collects, completely stripping away its investment returns (like real
estate rent or T-Bond interest).
\[[[1]{.underline}](https://einvestingforbeginners.com/combined-ratio-aher/),
[[2]{.underline}](https://www.insuranceandestates.com/mutual-insurance-company-vs-stock-insurance-company/)\]

A combined ratio below 100% indicates underwriting profitability, while
a ratio above 100% means the company is losing money on its insurance
policies and relying entirely on its investment portfolio to stay
afloat.
\[[[1]{.underline}](https://insurancetrainingcenter.com/resource/loss-ratio/),
[[2]{.underline}](https://vivaldigroup.com/insurance-companies-can-transform-value-creation/),
[[3]{.underline}](https://www.valueresearchonline.com/stories/200634/does-high-turnover-matter/)\]

\\(\\text{Combined\\ Ratio}=\\text{Loss\\ Ratio}+\\text{Expense\\
Ratio}\\)

1.  **Loss Ratio:** Measures claims health.\
    \\(\\text{Loss\\ Ratio}=\\frac{\\text{Incurred\\ Claims\\ +\\
    Claims\\ Adjustment\\ Expenses}}{\\text{Net\\ Earned\\
    Premiums}}\\times 100\\)

2.  **Expense Ratio:** Measures administrative efficiency.\
    \\(\\text{Expense\\ Ratio}=\\frac{\\text{Underwriting\\ Expenses\\
    +\\ Commissions\\ +\\ Salaries}}{\\text{Net\\ Earned\\
    Premiums}}\\times 100\\)
    \[[[1]{.underline}](https://insurancetrainingcenter.com/resource/loss-ratio/),
    [[2]{.underline}](https://digisure.ai/blog/insurance-loss-ratio-the-metric-sharing-economy-companies-must-track),
    [[3]{.underline}](https://www.insurancebusinessmag.com/uk/glossary/loss-ratio-563012.aspx),
    [[4]{.underline}](https://www.meegle.com/en_us/topics/insurance/combined-ratio)\]

Combined Ratio \< 95%: Excellent Profitability (Strong margin of safety)

Combined Ratio 95%-99%: Acceptable (Lean operational margins)

Combined Ratio \> 100%: Underwriting Loss (Relies heavily on
bond/property markets)

-   **The Sub-100% Safe Zone (e.g., 92%):** For every KSh 100 the
    insurance company collects in premiums, it pays out KSh 92 in total
    claims and expenses. The remaining KSh 8 is pure underwriting
    profit. This bankable cash means the company\'s dividend is highly
    insulated, and its massive investment portfolio remains untouched to
    compound value over time.

-   **The Red Flag Zone (e.g., 106%):** For every KSh 100 collected, the
    company pays out KSh 106 in claims and administration. It is losing

**Investor Takeaway:** If an insurance company\'s combined ratio stays

hits the Nairobi Securities Exchange or real estate market, their

**2. Evaluating Working Capital Cycles: Manufacturing vs. Banking**

Working Capital measures a company\'s structural operational liquidity.
Comparing the working capital cycles of manufacturing counters against
banking counters reveals why you cannot evaluate their cash health using
the same rules.

Manufacturing Cycle: \[Cash\] -\> \[Buy Raw Materials\] -\> \[Build
Inventory\] -\> \[Sell on Credit\] -\> \[Collect Cash\]

Banking Cycle: \[Receive Customer Deposits\] -\> \[Issue Short/Long Term
Loans\] -\> \[Collect Interest\]

**A. The Manufacturing Working Capital Cycle (The Cash Conversion
Cycle)**

Manufacturing giants (BAT, EABL) rely on a physical asset timeline
measured by the **Cash Conversion Cycle (CCC)**:

\\(\\text{CCC}=\\text{Days\\ Inventory\\ Outstanding\\
(DIO)}+\\text{Days\\ Sales\\ Outstanding\\ (DSO)}-\\text{Days\\
Payable\\ Outstanding\\ (DPO)}\\)

-   **The Cash Health Indicator:** You want to see a short or
    negative CCC. For example, if a manufacturer takes 30 days to sell
    inventory (DIO), collects cash from supermarkets in 45 days (DSO),
    but negotiates 60 days to pay suppliers (DPO), their cash gap is
    only 15 days (\\(30 + 45 - 60\\)).
    \[[[1]{.underline}](https://www.bajajfinserv.in/what-is-the-cash-conversion-cycle),
    [[2]{.underline}](https://study.com/academy/lesson/operating-cycle-in-accounting-definition-formula.html),
    [[3]{.underline}](https://corporatefinanceinstitute.com/resources/financial-modeling/negative-working-capital-analysis/),
    [[4]{.underline}](https://www.crestmontcapital.com/blog/how-to-calculate-your-working-capital-needs)\]

-   **The Trap:** If supermarkets delay payments (DSO spikes to 90
    days), the working capital cycle stretches. Cash gets trapped on
    balance sheet invoices, starving the company of the actual liquid
    funds required to wire dividends.
    \[[[1]{.underline}](https://www.winvesta.in/blog/investors/working-capital-analysis-signs-of-a-healthy-business),
    [[2]{.underline}](https://www.qoyod.com/en/glossary/working-capital-cycle/)\]

**B. The Banking Counter Cycle (Asset-Liability Mismatch)**

**Banks do not have raw materials or physical inventory.** Their working
capital is purely financial liquidity, measured by the **Asset-Liability
Maturity Profile** found in the PDF disclosure notes.
\[[[1]{.underline}](https://ramp.com/blog/working-capital-vs-profit)\]

-   **The Cash Health Indicator:** Instead of physical tracking, a
    bank\'s cash health depends on matching the maturity durations of
    its liabilities (customer deposits) against its assets (loans).
    \[[[1]{.underline}](https://www.economyinsights.com/p/how-capital-structure-decisions-influence-the-financial-health-of-a-corporation)\]

-   **The Balance Dynamic:** Most customer deposits on the NSE are
    short-term or demand deposits (withdrawable instantly). However, the
    bank uses that money to issue 5-year corporate loans or buy 10-year
    government bonds.

-   **The Trap:** If a bank mismanages this cycle, it experiences a
    maturity mismatch. If panicked depositors withdraw cash quickly
    while the bank\'s capital is locked in long-term loans, the bank
    faces a liquidity crisis. They will instantly suspend dividend
    payouts to lock down cash reserves, even if their accounting profits
    look perfectly stable.
