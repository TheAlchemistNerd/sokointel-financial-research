# Part 12 — Nakumatt and Tuskys: two retail distress records

*Revised practical article; source details and figures are dated to their cited filing or event, with current evidence reviewed through 24 September 2026.*

Nakumatt and Tuskys are often discussed together as Kenyan supermarket failures. They are useful cases for asking what went wrong in a cash-intensive retail model, but their court records do not tell one interchangeable story. Each had its own legal company, creditor list, financing, operating history, and process. A retrospective that treats both as one event loses the facts that matter to an owner or lender.

The first step is to identify the legal entity behind each brand:

* **Nakumatt Holdings Limited** was placed under administration by a High Court order on 22 January 2018, according to the court’s 2021 ruling [1].
* **Tusker Mattresses Limited** operated the supermarket chain using the Tuskys name, according to the High Court judgment of 31 May 2023 [2]. That judgment issued an interim liquidation order and appointed a provisional liquidator.

Administration and liquidation are different legal stages. Administration can be used to pursue a rescue or a better outcome for creditors. Liquidation involves winding up the company under the applicable process. One company’s status cannot be inferred from the other’s.

## What the Nakumatt court record says

In March 2021, the High Court considered the administrator’s update report dated 1 February 2021. The judgment states that the administrator reported having raised KSh 5,218,737,409 and paid more than KSh 3.5 billion to creditors, including KSh 766,641,309 to landlords [1]. It then summarizes the administrator’s belief that Nakumatt had no property that might allow a distribution to creditors, while debts and legal cases remained to be managed. The court extended the administration to allow the administrator to carry out tasks in the report and disapplied parts of the statutory process.

This is an important distinction. The court’s summary of the administrator’s report is evidence of what the report stated. It is not a complete audited cash-flow history for the retailer. The judgment does not tell us what the business’s average cash conversion cycle was in each pre-administration year, what every asset sold for, or the final recovery received by each creditor. Nor does the reference to “no property” mean that all legal claims had ended. The court allowed administration to continue because matters remained to be managed.

For an owner, the practical question is what records would have shown the pressure before the court process: store-by-store contribution after rent and logistics, inventory ageing and shrinkage, supplier days, rent arrears, customer/supplier settlement timing, borrowing maturity, and uncommitted cash. The case materials reviewed here do not provide a complete contemporaneous data set for each of those measures. The article should not invent one.

## What the Tuskys judgment says

The May 2023 judgment consolidated three creditor petitions. The petitioners stated that Tuskys owed them KSh 248,220,797.38, KSh 11,284,096.50, and KSh 30,892,518.92 respectively [2]. Those are amounts claimed by the petitioning creditors, not automatically the total amount ultimately owed to every creditor. The court considered the company’s response and the period in which it had been given an opportunity to restructure. The judge concluded that the company had failed to demonstrate a viable prospect of maintaining solvency, allowed the petitions, issued an interim liquidation order, and appointed a provisional liquidator.

The judgment is a strong source for that legal outcome and the positions placed before the court. It should not be used to state an exact recovery for all suppliers or workers. A later 2025 ruling in the insolvency matter records the interim liquidator seeking additional time to engage a forensic auditor and complete the statement of affairs [3]. A request in court is not the same thing as the final result of an audit or a completed liquidation. Any article published later should check for subsequent court orders and the liquidator’s latest report.

## The working-capital questions retail owners can use

A supermarket pays suppliers, collects from household shoppers, holds a wide stock range, and carries rent, payroll, security, power, delivery and distribution costs. Cash pressure can come from several directions at once:

* inventory is purchased before it sells;
* supplier terms shorten while customer payment terms remain long;
* slow-moving goods and shrinkage consume cash;
* low-margin promotions generate volume but not enough contribution after handling costs;
* a store adds fixed rent and payroll before it reaches productive volume;
* debt repayment dates fail to match a seasonal trading cycle;
* supplier or landlord arrears reduce the firm’s ability to restock or keep locations open.

Working-capital research reports relationships between receivable, inventory and payable policies and firm performance in defined samples [4], [5]. Those studies motivate measurement, not a claim that one ratio explains a specific retailer’s administration or liquidation. A better operating dashboard would calculate contribution by store and product group, track inventory ageing and shrinkage, compare purchase volumes with sell-through, and show the 13-week cash trough after actual supplier and rent dates.

A negative or shortening cash-conversion cycle can provide some funding if suppliers allow it. It becomes fragile if the business relies on ever-longer overdue payables. A supplier invoice unpaid past its agreed terms is not cheap financing. It is a creditor claim and can damage the supply the business needs to trade.

## The retrospective boundary

The public court records establish significant distress, creditor claims, and different legal outcomes. They do not let us confidently attribute each company’s decline to one cause, calculate its last normalized owner earnings, or estimate what shareholders would have recovered without the insolvency file and full statements of affairs. Company history, store closures, family ownership or debt headlines can suggest questions. They are not a substitute for audited accounts, contemporaneous management records and administrator reports.

A warning model could preserve the timing of changes rather than classify each retailer from one isolated year; hazard models are one approach used in public-company distress research [6]. That literature does not supply a ready-made threshold for Kenyan supermarkets. A rigorous next stage would request or locate the relevant audited statements, administrator reports, creditor statements, insolvency orders, and any liquidator statement of affairs. Then an analyst could build a timeline using only contemporaneous information up to each warning date and compare it with what the later record revealed. Until those records are compiled, the right finding is bounded: these retail companies entered severe distress; the court records support particular dated legal and creditor facts; detailed pre-failure cash-flow causation remains unresolved.

## How to turn the cases into a testable retail review

A board or lender reviewing a supermarket should put the operating statements next to the cash calendar. Start with sales by store and category, then subtract landed product cost, promotions, shrinkage, payment costs, delivery and store-level payroll. Rent, central distribution and head-office costs need an explicit allocation rule. A store can report positive gross profit and still consume cash after rent, utilities, security and logistics. Conversely, a new store may be temporarily cash-negative while reaching a viable sales base; the records should show whether the ramp was planned and funded.

For inventory, compare purchase receipts with point-of-sale sell-through, age stock by product and store, and mark damaged, expired or slow-moving items separately. For payables, reconcile supplier statements to the ledger and contract terms; an unpaid invoice is not automatically an agreed extension. For rent, list arrears and the next contractual dates by location. Then combine the figures in a weekly forecast that includes payroll, tax, debt payments and realistic supplier settlement dates. These records would help test a cash-cycle explanation. They cannot be imputed to Nakumatt or Tuskys without their underlying data.

Consider a clearly hypothetical store: it earns KSh 10 million in monthly sales, but its gross margin falls by two percentage points while rent and payroll remain fixed. The first response should not be “cut inventory” in the abstract. Management would check whether the margin decline comes from discounts, category mix, supplier cost, waste or pricing; then estimate the cash effect of each action. A blanket stock cut could worsen availability and sales. The useful warning is the bridge between a measured change, its likely cause, and the option that preserves positive contribution.

## A dated evidence ledger for the public cases

The next researcher can make the court-record work reproducible with one row per assertion. Record the quoted statement, the named speaker, legal company, document date, court or administrator status, and whether the amount is a claim, an estimate, a payment, or an adjudicated liability. Keep a separate “known by decision date” view. Nakumatt’s 2021 ruling summarizes a 2021 administrator update; that is valuable evidence of the later process, but not a substitute for a 2016 or 2017 management forecast. The Tuskys petition amounts belong to the petitioners’ assertions as summarized in a 2023 judgment; they should not be added and labelled as all company debt.

A strong later version could connect those ledger rows to audited statements, lease schedules, borrowing documents, inventory records, administrator reports and the liquidator’s statement of affairs. It could then test competing explanations: weak store economics, working-capital stress, financing constraints, governance, or some combination. Until those documents are located, the responsible result is an evidence-bounded comparison of two legal proceedings, not a ranking of which retailer failed “worse.”

## Compare the records without turning them into one story

Nakumatt and Tuskys help an owner study two parts of retail distress: the operating cash cycle and the legal process that follows when obligations cannot be met. The materials currently cited show different formal proceedings and different windows into the companies. Nakumatt entered administration in January 2018; the 2021 ruling later summarized an administrator update, creditor payments and tasks that remained [1]. Tusker Mattresses faced consolidated creditor petitions and a 2023 interim liquidation order; a 2025 ruling later recorded the interim liquidator's request for time to engage a forensic auditor and complete the statement of affairs [2, 3].

The distinction changes what a reader can safely infer. A court record may identify the entity, procedural stage, dates, creditor claims and directions. It may not provide a clean history of every store's economics or all claims admitted. A later request for forensic work suggests that the financial reconstruction was still in progress at that point; it does not tell us what the final report concluded. A retrospective should update the status when a later court order or liquidator report becomes available, while keeping earlier statements in their original date context.

The two records should therefore be kept in separate case folders. Do not combine petition amounts, administrator receipts, store counts, assets or creditor recoveries unless each source establishes that they cover comparable scopes. Do not treat the same brand, supplier or landlord as proof that the legal estates are the same. If a creditor traded with both companies, record each contract and claim against the correct entity.

## Reconstruct the retail engine from transactions

Retail operates on thin timing margins. The store collects quickly from many shoppers but pays a series of suppliers, landlords, employees and logistics providers. The gap can provide low-cost operating finance when the supplier terms are agreed and sustainable. It can become a source of distress when stock turns slow, invoices are disputed, terms are shortened or overdue amounts accumulate.

For a retailer with adequate records, calculate an approximate cash conversion cycle:

* **Inventory days:** average inventory divided by cost of goods sold, multiplied by the period's days.
* **Receivable days:** average trade receivables divided by credit sales, multiplied by the period's days.
* **Payable days:** average trade payables divided by purchases on credit, multiplied by the period's days.
* **Cash conversion cycle:** inventory days plus receivable days less payable days.

The ratios are only diagnostic. Use the same entity, period and accounting basis. If credit sales or supplier purchases are not disclosed, explain the proxy. A supermarket with substantial till sales may have a very low receivable balance, while a wholesaler can have a long customer collection cycle. Trade payables must be compared with credit purchases, not automatically with cost of sales, especially when inventory and purchases are changing quickly.

Imagine, purely as an operating example, a retailer that sells KSh 20 million each week, incurs KSh 17 million of weekly product cost, holds KSh 68 million of average stock and owes suppliers KSh 51 million for purchases on 30-day terms. Inventory is about four weeks of cost and payables are about three weeks of cost. Even before rent, payroll, taxes, shrinkage and other obligations, roughly one week of product cost is tied up in the cycle. If slow lines rise by KSh 10 million, supplier terms fall from 30 to 14 days, and rent is due before the weekend's receipts settle, the liquidity gap can widen sharply without an immediate decline in reported annual revenue.

The example is not a claim about Nakumatt or Tuskys. It shows why a yearly profit number is not enough to reconstruct a retailer's cash position. For each month, an analyst would want daily or weekly cash receipts, supplier due dates, inventory receipts and sales, store-level contribution, rent schedule, payroll and borrowing. Where those records are absent from the public file, the article should present the measurement design and state that case-specific attribution remains unknown.

## Distinguish profitable sales from cash-consuming growth

A retailer can grow sales by adding stores, extending credit to wholesale customers, widening the assortment or discounting to increase basket size. Each choice changes cash needs. A new location creates deposits, fit-out, opening inventory, staff and rent before reaching a stable volume. A promotion can lift sales and still destroy contribution if discount, handling and shrinkage exceed the incremental margin. More SKU variety can improve customer choice but increase slow stock and replenishment complexity.

A store-level contribution report should start with net sales after discounts and returns, then subtract landed product cost, direct store payroll, payment fees, packaging, delivery, shrinkage, utilities and rent. Show central distribution and head-office allocation separately so that the reader can see the allocation rule. Then compare the store's contribution with opening costs, refurbishment and working-capital requirements. If a location is consistently negative, decide whether it is in a funded ramp period, strategically necessary, or structurally uneconomic. Avoid allowing a profitable flagship to conceal chronic losses at another site.

Category analysis should follow margin and cash turn together. A fast-selling item with a small contribution can still be valuable if it brings customers who buy higher-margin products; a high-margin product may be poor cash if it sits for months or spoils. Track gross contribution per shelf or unit of inventory, markdowns, write-offs, returns, promotions, stock-outs and supplier rebates. Verify that rebates are earned, collectible and not already netted against purchase cost.

## Map supplier arrears and landlord exposure precisely

A payable schedule should separate current invoices, invoices within agreed terms, disputed amounts, overdue invoices, supplier advances and balances under a signed rescheduling agreement. Reconcile supplier statements to the general ledger and obtain confirmations for the largest balances. A payment delay is not automatically an agreed extension. If the business has exceeded terms, identify which suppliers have stopped deliveries or changed from credit to cash on delivery.

For landlords, list location, legal lessor, rent due date, arrears, deposit, service charges, renewal, break rights, guarantee and any court or termination notice. Store closure may reduce future cash burn but trigger lease claims, employee costs, stock transfers and customer disruption. Compare the cost of closing with the cash contribution and exit obligations of continuing. A retailer that delays rent may preserve immediate liquidity while weakening its ability to keep the sites essential to its sales network.

The accounts payable and lease files also matter to a failure retrospective because they reveal the order of events. If a supplier first requested payment, later stopped deliveries and only then filed a claim, each date belongs in the timeline. If a landlord petition states an amount, record it as a claim until there is an adjudicated or agreed balance. Never turn an aggregate creditor claim into an exact measure of the company's total obligations without evidence.

## Use a weekly rescue review before a legal crisis

An owner or board facing a cash squeeze should meet weekly with finance, merchandising, operations and collections. The first page is a 13-week cash forecast with unrestricted opening cash and named receipts by expected date. The second page lists stock by ageing and criticality, suppliers whose terms changed, rent or tax obligations due, debt maturities and orders that require a deposit. The meeting then decides which spending can be stopped, which collections need escalation, which suppliers require a transparent schedule, and what financing or restructuring conversation must begin.

Every action should be tested for its effect on total economics:

* Will a purchasing freeze prevent stock-out of high-contribution items?
* Will a promotion convert slow inventory into cash after discount, fees and returns?
* Does a supplier extension have written consent and preserve supply?
* Does a location closure save rent and payroll after exit and relocation costs?
* Does a new loan finance a defined recovery or only postpone recurring losses?
* Does new equity or an owner injection fund a viable operation or meet old arrears without a turnaround plan?

The board should establish approval limits and a named person who can update the forecast. If the same owner controls sales, purchasing, supplier setup and payment, add independent review of bank reconciliations, supplier master changes and large disbursements. Weak cash controls can hide error and fraud as well as ordinary timing problems.

## What the case evidence can and cannot teach

The court materials in this article establish dated legal findings and attributed financial claims; they do not provide the monthly transactional records needed for a complete operating model [1–3]. Peer-reviewed studies of working capital and SME profitability support examining receivable, inventory and payable policies, but their samples and settings do not identify the cause of either Kenyan retailer's distress [4, 5]. Shumway's hazard model shows why distress analysis should consider timing and changing variables rather than classify a company from one annual ratio [6]. The practical lesson is to build a time-aware reconstruction from primary records before deciding whether growth, governance, credit, supplier terms or store economics was decisive.

A useful conclusion can be both bounded and practical: the legal records show that two major supermarket entities reached formal distress processes; their cash-cycle drivers are testable; and the cited public materials do not yet establish a full pre-distress monthly cause-and-effect story. A retailer can use the dashboard, cash forecast, entity map and source ledger now. A researcher can update the historical account when the official statement of affairs or a later court order becomes available.

## A more useful cash comparison than a final-year headline

A disciplined retailer analysis would compare at least three levels of evidence. First, store and category contribution explains whether sales covered direct product, service and location costs. Second, the group cash bridge explains whether the business funded stock, rent, staff, tax and debt on time. Third, the insolvency record explains what the administrator or liquidator later reported about assets, claims, payments and remaining tasks. A strong result at one level does not answer the other two.

A final-year profit can include noncash valuation movements or accruals, while a final-year cash receipt can come from new debt, sale proceeds or a temporary supplier stretch. A retailer with a negative operating contribution cannot be rescued indefinitely by delaying invoices. But an unpaid supplier balance alone does not tell us whether the stores could have been viable with new equity, a sale, fewer locations or better purchasing terms. The retrospective needs both an operating and financing counterfactual.

A case table can make that boundary plain:

| Question | Evidence that would answer it | What the public judgments cited here establish |
|---|---|---|
| Were stores contributing after rent and direct costs? | Store-level management accounts and lease schedules | Not available in the cited rulings |
| How large and old was inventory? | Stock ledger, counts, write-offs and administrator valuation | Not a complete SKU or age profile |
| What was owed to creditors? | Verified statement of affairs, admitted claims and security | Particular claims and administrator-reported payments, not a universal final balance |
| What cash arrived from asset or store disposals? | Bank records and administrator realization statement | Some administrator-reported collections for Nakumatt; not a full disposition ledger |
| What could owners have changed earlier? | Contemporaneous forecasts, board records, contracts and supplier terms | Not established by the court summaries alone |

The table helps a reader distinguish a limitation of public evidence from a claim that the records do not exist. A further research step is to obtain or locate the administrator's update reports, liquidation statements, audited accounts and formal creditor schedules, then match each to the correct proceeding.
