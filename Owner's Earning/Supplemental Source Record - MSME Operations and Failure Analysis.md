## Capture A: questions owner earnings should answer

### Author question

What different questions can Buffett's owner-earnings concept answer for MSMEs and for listed-company valuation across industries and asset profiles?

### Substantive ideas captured

- The central survival question is why reported profit can coexist with an empty bank account. The proposed explanation is that inventory, receivables and necessary upkeep absorb cash before it can be distributed.
- The working-capital question is how much cash must remain committed to the supply chain, particularly when growth increases receivables and inventory faster than internally generated cash.
- The distribution question is how much cash an owner may remove without impairing operating capacity or competitive position.
- The exit question is what sustainable cash earning power a buyer is acquiring. The capture links this to Seller's Discretionary Earnings (SDE), but the research must keep SDE and owner earnings distinct and reconcile them explicitly.
- For asset-heavy businesses, the proposed questions concern whether accounting depreciation reflects the current cost of maintaining or replacing productive assets, and how much capital expenditure merely keeps the business in place.
- For asset-light businesses, the proposed questions concern how much revenue becomes deployable cash, how much reinvestment is hidden in people or intangible activity, and whether growth depends on debt or dilution.
- Proposed sector adjustments include stock-based compensation and dilution for technology; structural upkeep and property-specific cash measures for real estate; seasonal inventory and wholesale receivables for retail and e-commerce; and regulatory capital rather than physical capital expenditure for banks and other financial institutions.

### Formula captured, with correction required

The supplied response presented a simplified expression:

\[
\text{Owner earnings}
= \text{net income}
+ \text{depreciation and amortisation}
- \text{maintenance capital expenditure}
\pm \Delta\text{working capital}.
\]

For the programme, this is only a shorthand. The research must return to Buffett's original formulation, identify other non-cash charges, distinguish maintenance working-capital requirements from temporary releases, and define whether the resulting claim belongs to the firm or equity holders. The use of a free-standing plus-or-minus sign for working capital is a captured claim requiring correction rather than an adopted rule.

## Capture B: owner earnings, the cash conversion cycle and hindsight failure analysis

### Author proposition

The “cash flow is king” idea is tied to the cash conversion cycle (CCC), and businesses such as Nakumatt and Twiga can be examined retrospectively through owner earnings and the CCC.

### Analytical proposition retained

Owner earnings and CCC can be used together as a diagnostic lens:

- owner earnings asks whether operations produce sustainable cash after necessary reinvestment;
- Days Sales Outstanding (DSO), Days Inventory Outstanding (DIO) and Days Payables Outstanding (DPO) locate where operating cash is tied up or temporarily financed;
- funding runway asks how long external capital can cover the gap;
- governance and allocation analysis asks who authorised expansion, related-party activity, credit terms or supplier delays, and whether controls worked;
- supplier and customer behaviour tests whether the apparent financing model depends on continued supplier tolerance, customer prepayments, loose customer credit or uninterrupted volume.

The combined analysis must not assume that an adverse outcome proves earlier owner earnings were negative. It must reconstruct the record by period and separate operating losses, working-capital absorption, maintenance investment, growth investment, financing cash flows and governance events.

### Nakumatt claims captured for verification

The supplied response proposes the following failure narrative. Every item below is an unverified research lead:

- a supermarket would ordinarily collect cash rapidly, carry inventory for a limited period and receive some supplier credit, potentially producing a short or negative CCC;
- aggressive expansion allegedly increased slow-moving stock and store-fit-out requirements;
- supplier payments were allegedly delayed to finance expansion, after which reduced supplier support contributed to empty shelves and falling sales;
- the response mentions more than 60 stores, an alleged KES 18 billion inventory “black hole”, peak gross revenue of KES 52.2 billion, debt and commercial-paper dependence, and administration or closure dates around 2018–2020;
- the response concludes that owner earnings were negative and insolvency preceded closure, but those conclusions require dated financial records, administrator or court materials and a reproducible cash bridge.

### Twiga Foods claims captured for verification

The supplied response proposes a different failure or distress narrative. Every item below is an unverified research lead:

- a technology-enabled agricultural distributor may carry perishables, warehouses, logistics fleets and fuel exposure, making its physical operating model more capital intensive than the “tech” label suggests;
- spoilage and slow movement could increase the economic cost of inventory;
- customer credit to small retailers could lengthen DSO, while farmers and other suppliers might need faster payment;
- the response mentions more than USD 185 million of funding, an approximate KES 24 billion conversion, cold rooms, warehouses, logistics fleets, negative unit economics and a venture-capital funding slowdown;
- it also states that Twiga entered statutory administration and that negative owner earnings became fatal. These are especially material legal and causal claims and must not be repeated without direct confirmation. The retrospective must allow for the possibility that public evidence supports distress, restructuring or layoffs but not the specific legal status or causal chain asserted by the AI response.

### Comparative model suggested by the capture

The material suggests a sequence worth testing rather than assuming:

1. recurring operating and reinvestment cash deficits;
2. deficits covered by equity funding, debt, customer advances or extended supplier credit;
3. deterioration in DSO, DIO, DPO or gross contribution by cohort, route, store or product;
4. a governance or control failure that permits the gap to persist or remain hidden;
5. loss of funder, supplier or customer confidence;
6. a liquidity event, retrenchment, restructuring, administration or closure.

## Capture D: daily MSME operating toolkit

The additional material argues that owner earnings is a periodic measure of sustainable economic cash generation, while managers need a more frequent operating dashboard. The following items are adopted as research and workbook requirements; any universal threshold mentioned in the source remains a hypothesis to verify and adapt by business.

### Liquidity and survival

- **Thirteen-week rolling cash forecast:** map weekly opening cash, collections and other inflows against payroll, suppliers, tax, debt service, rent, licences and other outflows; roll the horizon forward each week; show the lowest cash week and the size and timing of any funding gap.
- **Gross burn, net burn and runway:** define the cash basis and period; distinguish recurring operating burn from one-off or growth spending; calculate how many months current unrestricted cash can fund the selected burn scenario; handle zero or positive net burn without misleading division.
- **Current assets and current liabilities:** use balance-sheet liquidity measures as context, while avoiding the captured claim that a single current-ratio band is ideal for every industry.

### Unit economics and break-even

- **Contribution margin:** revenue less costs that vary with the unit, order, route or customer; show both value and percentage and document the cost classification.
- **Break-even:** fixed cash operating costs divided by contribution per unit, or fixed costs divided by contribution-margin ratio for revenue break-even; extend to a cash break-even that includes required debt service, tax and maintenance spending where appropriate.
- **Customer acquisition cost and lifetime value:** retain as an optional growth diagnostic, but reject a universal 3:1 rule unless the business model, payback period, churn, gross margin and evidence support it.

### Receivables and collection

- **A/R aging:** invoice-level buckets for current, 1–30, 31–60, 61–90 and more than 90 days past due, with disputed, promised, concentrated and credit-insured amounts separately visible.
- **DSO:** use credit sales, not indiscriminate total sales, and match the sales period to the receivable balance. Compare with contractual terms and cohort-level collection patterns.
- **Credit controls:** document credit approval, limits, references or other evidence, order holds, exceptions and override authority.
- **Collection actions proposed in the capture:** invoice promptly, offer suitable digital payment rails, send reminders before and after due dates, escalate contact, consider economically justified early-payment discounts, and apply contractual late fees or stop-supply rules only after legal, customer-relationship and operational review.
- The captured examples “2/10 net 30”, 1.5%–2% monthly late fees, a 45- or 60-day stop-supply point and a 15-day call escalation are examples to test, not programme defaults.

### Inventory and purchasing

- **Inventory aging:** track receipt date, last sale, last movement, expiry or obsolescence exposure, quantity, unit cost, current recoverable value and provision status.
- **ABC classification:** rank inventory by an explicit measure such as annual consumption value, gross contribution or revenue; do not hard-code the captured 80/15/5 revenue and 20/30/50 stock percentages as universal facts.
- **Turnover and DIO:** calculate COGS divided by average inventory and its days equivalent, using consistent periods and average balances.
- **Stock-to-sales and sales velocity:** compare inventory available with recent sales, while controlling for seasonality, lead times, stock-outs and expected promotions.
- **Holding cost:** model financing, space, handling, insurance, security, shrinkage, spoilage and obsolescence. The captured 20%–30% annual range is a lead, not a default benchmark.
- **Actions proposed in the capture:** bundle slow stock with faster items, negotiate returns or swaps, use staged markdowns or cost-recovery sales, write off unrecoverable stock under documented accounting and tax rules, and set reorder points from demand, lead time and safety-stock evidence.
- The proposed 60–90 day dead-stock rule and generic “just in time” policy must be adapted to shelf life, replenishment risk and customer service requirements.

### Payables and the complete cash conversion cycle

Although the daily-tool responses concentrated on receivables and inventory, the failure material makes DPO and supplier behaviour essential. The operating toolkit must therefore include:

- an A/P aging ledger separating contractual current balances, disputed items and overdue balances;
- DPO calculated consistently with purchases or an explained COGS proxy;
- supplier concentration, criticality, credit limits, stopped-delivery status and dependency flags;
- the relationship `CCC = DSO + DIO - DPO`, interpreted with the underlying ledgers rather than as a stand-alone target;
- warnings where a falling CCC results from distressed non-payment rather than stronger operations.

## Capture E: proposed operational responses and their limits

The source proposes a sequence of visibility, triage and prevention:

1. identify the bottleneck through the cash forecast, aging ledgers and unit economics;
2. assign an owner and dated action to the affected invoice, stock item, supplier balance or spending line;
3. quantify the cash released, margin surrendered and relationship or legal cost of the action;
4. update the rolling forecast and runway after the action;
5. change credit, purchasing, pricing, reorder or approval rules to prevent recurrence.

This sequence is retained. Specific actions such as refusing further supply, charging interest, discounting below cost, returning goods or claiming tax relief require contract, consumer-protection, competition, tax, accounting and relationship review in the relevant jurisdiction.

### Queue 1 — definitions and formula integrity

- Retrieve Buffett's 1986 letter and preserve the exact owner-earnings definition and caveats.

### Queue 2 — daily operating benchmarks

- Verify the appropriate construction and limitations of a thirteen-week cash forecast.
- Verify burn and runway formulas for positive, zero and negative burn cases.
- Research sector-specific evidence for A/R aging actions, DSO, DIO, DPO, holding-cost components, ABC methods, reorder points and bad-debt or stock provisions.
- Treat every numeric rule in the capture—3:1 LTV:CAC, 1.5–2.0 current ratio, 20%–30% holding cost, 60–90 day dead-stock trigger, 2/10 net 30, 1.5%–2% monthly late fee and 45/60-day stop-supply point—as unverified until a context-specific source and decision rationale are recorded.

### Queue 3 — Nakumatt and Tuskys

- Build dated event timelines from court, administrator, creditor, regulator and company records.
- Reconstruct available revenue, margins, operating cash flow, inventory, receivables, payables, borrowing, capital expenditure, store count and related-party or governance events.
- Test whether supplier-credit expansion preceded inventory withdrawal and quantify the extent possible.
- Mark unavailable records and avoid back-solving precise owner earnings from unsupported totals.

### Queue 4 — Twiga and startup selection

- Establish Twiga's verified legal and operating status for each relevant date.
- Build a funding-round register from company and investor announcements, then reconcile amounts and currencies.
- Seek primary evidence for inventory ownership, spoilage, credit terms, owned versus leased infrastructure, unit economics, layoffs or restructuring and supplier/customer conduct.
- Score Copia and Zumi on primary-evidence availability, financial comparability, operational contrast with Twiga and legal clarity. Select only the better-supported candidate; record why the other was not chosen.

### Queue 5 — comparative warning framework

- Define comparable monthly or annual signals for owner earnings, CCC components, cash runway, debt service, covenant headroom, supplier arrears, customer concentration, inventory provisions and governance overrides.
- Separate early warnings visible in contemporaneous records from conclusions available only in hindsight.
- Record false positives and alternative explanations so the framework does not imply that one weak ratio predicts failure.
