# Part 04 — Owner pay, reserves, growth and debt

*Revised practical article; source details and figures are dated to their cited filing or event, with current evidence reviewed through 24 September 2026.*

Owners often fund two very different things from one bank account: the work they do and the capital they put at risk. If the owner manages staff every day, their pay for that job is an operating cost. A dividend or draw is a return on ownership. Mixing the two can make a business appear more profitable than it is and can make the owner think a draw is “salary” when it is actually draining working capital.

The first step is to set a reasonable replacement cost for the owner’s work. Ask what duties are performed, how many hours they take, which credentials are required, and what it would cost to hire someone capable of doing them. There may be more than one role: founder sales, purchasing, bookkeeping, repair work, and general management. Do not invent a precise salary from a single online job listing. Document the role and use a range. For a sole proprietor, the bookkeeping or tax treatment may differ from a company’s payroll, so keep tax classification separate from the economic normalization used for analysis.

## A reserve belongs to a named risk

“Keep three months of expenses” is a common shortcut. It may be too little for a seasonal importer with long lead times, and too much for a service firm paid in advance with low fixed costs. A reserve should be linked to what could go wrong and how quickly the owner can respond.

List obligations by date: payroll, rent, taxes, key suppliers, loan payments, insurance, and customer deposits that must be refunded if delivery fails. Then identify the risks that could delay receipts: one large buyer, a public-sector payment cycle, FX exposure, weather, a supplier outage, a licensing renewal, or an owner’s illness. The reserve question is not “how many months sounds prudent?” It is “what cash shortfall could the business face before it can change course?”

A useful reserve schedule shows a base case and at least one plausible downside. For example, move a major customer’s expected payment from week 3 to week 7, keep payroll on its actual date, and show the trough. If the business has a committed overdraft, show its limit, conditions, amount already used, expiry date, price, and collateral. Do not count an unapproved facility as cash.

## Growth is not always self-funding

A new order can increase profit and worsen cash at the same time. Suppose a fictional wholesaler wins a KSh 900,000 order. The buyer pays after delivery and acceptance in 45 days. The wholesaler must pay KSh 520,000 for stock in two weeks and KSh 80,000 for transport and handling before delivery. The order appears to have a KSh 300,000 gross spread before overhead. But the business needs KSh 600,000 of cash for more than a month before it collects. A deposit, supplier credit, or working-capital facility changes the funding need. None changes the customer’s gross margin by itself.

Before borrowing, write the order’s dates and identify what a lender will actually fund: purchase invoices, a receivable, inventory, or general cash flow. Then check if the facility matures before the buyer is likely to pay. Make a downside case for delayed acceptance, a short delivery, disputed invoice, FX movement, or partial buyer payment. Ask whether the facility is a term loan, revolving credit, overdraft, invoice discounting, or a different contract. Names do not control; the agreement does.

Cash available for debt service should be defined from the actual financing documents. Lenders differ on EBITDA adjustments, taxes, maintenance spend, leases, and one-off items. “DSCR” can mean different things in different contracts. In an article or model, show the chosen formula and source. Do not call a generic ratio the borrower’s covenant without reading the agreement.

## What lenders and owners each need to know

A lender asks whether the borrower can repay under the contract and what recovery exists if it cannot. The owner asks whether capital invested produces enough risk-adjusted value and what can be withdrawn without impairing the business. Those questions overlap but are not identical.

Small-business finance research describes how financing sources change as firms grow and accumulate information, track records, and assets that support lending [1]. Classic credit-market research also shows why interest rates alone do not always clear credit demand when lenders have less information about borrower risk [2]. This helps explain why a well-intentioned owner may face documentation requests or a smaller credit limit than expected. The study does not prove that a particular lender has acted fairly or unfairly. The owner’s response is to make the business legible: reconcile bank inflows to invoices, keep statements and tax filings consistent, identify the legal borrower, and maintain an aged receivable schedule. Afrifa’s UK SME study also finds that cash flow changes how the working-capital/performance relationship is modeled, which supports keeping the reserve decision tied to dated cash needs rather than a balance-sheet ratio alone [3].

The owner can plan three different buckets:

* **Operating cash:** payments needed to deliver contracted work and trade normally.
* **Resilience reserve:** cash assigned to a stated risk and kept in a liquid, accessible account.
* **Potential distribution:** the remaining amount after due obligations, scheduled debt payments, and approved reinvestment.

If an owner wants to invest in growth, label it as an investment decision. Show the cash cost, when it might earn a return, what evidence supports demand, and what would make the decision wrong. Then test whether the business survives if the return arrives later or is smaller.

## A decision rule that can be explained

Before an owner distribution, ask:

1. Is the business’s historical cash bridge reconciled to statements and invoices?
2. Have we paid or set aside cash for obligations due before the next dependable receipts?
3. Have we funded the replacement and working capital needs of the existing operation?
4. Have we considered signed contracts and the exact loan covenants?
5. Does a plausible delay leave the business above its agreed cash floor?

If the last answer is no, the right decision may be to distribute less even when reported profit is positive. That does not mean the owner’s equity has no value. It means the value and distribution decision have different dates and liquidity tests.


## Work the debt decision from use to repayment

Before the business borrows, the owner should be able to point to the purchase, expected benefit and repayment source. For a working-capital line, map the cash cycle: when stock is paid, when it is sold, when the invoice is issued, and when the customer pays. For an asset loan, connect the loan term to the asset’s useful life and the cash it enables. If the use is “general support,” list the actual underlying obligations and decide whether they are temporary timing needs or recurring operating losses.

A borrower-to-lender bridge should define its starting measure. If starting from normalized EBITDA, deduct cash tax, maintenance investment, incremental operating working capital and other senior obligations to estimate pre-financing cash available for debt service. If starting from net income, interest and tax may already be included; do not subtract them again without explaining the adjustment. Add scheduled principal and interest using the signed facility terms. The bank may define DSCR differently, include or exclude certain add-backs, test quarterly or annually, and require minimum cash. Put the covenant definition and test date beside the illustrative ratio before using the model for a real loan.

For the fictional KSh 900,000 order, compare the same customer, supplier and delivery dates under a term loan and a revolver. Assume stock costs KSh 620,000 and delivery plus other variable costs KSh 90,000. The customer pays KSh 1,050,000 sixty days after delivery. Contribution before overhead and financing is KSh 340,000. Suppose a term loan carries 18% annual interest, 2% arrangement fee and six equal monthly principal repayments; the revolver carries 20% interest, a 1% draw fee and a 1% annual fee on undrawn commitment. These are hypothetical terms. The model should calculate draw date, interest days, fees, principal due, cash received and closing debt under each option. Then move customer acceptance or payment by 30 days. If the term loan amortizes before collection, cash may fall below reserve; if the revolver must clean down, the buyer's delay can prevent compliance. Do not compare headline rates alone.

The financing schedule should include required security, personal guarantees, floating or fixed rate, rate resets, unused-line fees, legal charges, covenant tests, borrowing base, clean-down days, renewal date, early repayment fee and default margin where applicable. A quote should be tied to a written offer. The actual contract governs. The workbook can indicate a model breach under selected assumptions; it cannot determine the lender's legal interpretation or waive a covenant.

## Decide whether extra equity is better than debt

Debt preserves ownership percentage but adds fixed claims. Equity can absorb uncertainty but dilutes the owner and may require governance rights or preferred returns. Compare the full cash and control effects. If the purchase funds a machine with predictable contracted work, debt may match a durable asset. If it funds product development with uncertain time to revenue, borrowing can create repayment pressure before evidence of demand exists. If the business is already undercapitalized, new equity may protect suppliers and staff while the owner proves whether the model works.

For each funding choice, record cash received net of fees, effective annual cost, principal timing, security, ownership dilution, control rights, repayment source and downside outcome. Include owner equity only when it is committed and available. An informal promise from a relative is not a bankable source until amount, date and terms are agreed. If a lender requires the owner to inject cash first, make that condition explicit in the forecast.

The correct decision may be smaller scope. A firm could lease a vehicle, outsource one route, require a customer deposit, stage equipment purchases, or test demand before hiring full-time. Compare the total cost of those options and who bears residual value and downtime risk. A lease can lower day-one cash need while creating a long payment obligation; it should appear in the same dated schedule as debt.

## Protect the business without starving it

Owner distributions, reinvestment and reserve policy compete for the same cash. The owner should agree a rule before year-end, such as paying a fixed monthly amount only while forecast closing cash remains above the reserve under base and downside cases. A year-end sweep can distribute surplus after tax, supplier reconciliations, maintenance approvals and seasonal working capital are confirmed. The rule should not guarantee a draw when invoices or stock needs change.

Use a cash waterfall:

1. Restricted money and customer obligations remain protected.
2. Payroll, tax, safety-critical suppliers and contractually due debt are funded.
3. Maintenance needed to continue current service is funded or explicitly scheduled.
4. The chosen operating reserve and seasonal working capital are preserved.
5. Committed growth investments are tested for incremental return.
6. Remaining cash can be considered for owner distribution or optional expansion.

The order can be adapted to legal requirements and company agreements, but the model should show the trade-offs. If debt covenants prohibit distributions, that condition overrides the discretionary waterfall. If the business has excess cash above a robust reserve, keeping it idle also has a cost; compare debt repayment, maintenance, profitable growth and owner use.

## A board-ready growth case

A growth case needs a no-growth reference. Forecast the existing operation first, including replacement capex and normal working capital. Then add only the incremental project: price, unit volume, contribution, new payroll, equipment, lease, launch costs, working-capital build, tax and funding. Do not apply the company’s historic average margin blindly if the new product, buyer or route has different economics.

Use three scenarios. In the base, state expected time to ramp and evidence such as signed orders or repeat customer demand. In the downside, delay opening, reduce volume and assume a customer pays later while the supplier withdraws terms. In the upside, avoid relying on immediate full capacity unless demand is contracted. Show cumulative cash invested, payback date, return on incremental capital and the minimum liquidity point. A project with positive total profit can still fail if cash is needed before the first collection.

Set a stop rule tied to observable milestones: minimum weekly orders, contribution per order, utilization or customer retention by a specific date. Release the next tranche only when evidence arrives. If the test fails, options might include reduce opening hours, change price, renegotiate a lease or stop the next equipment purchase. A stop rule protects the owner from continuing to spend solely because earlier spend is sunk.

## Revisit the policy as the business changes

Review reserve and borrowing assumptions when the largest customer changes terms, a supplier shortens credit, the tax calendar changes, the business hires a replacement manager, an asset needs unexpected work or loan rates reset. One annual budget cannot capture these events. Maintain a short debt maturity ladder with amounts due in 30, 90, 180 and 365 days, and begin renewal discussions before the facility expires. If the lender requires seasonal clean-down, identify the exact week the balance must return to zero and whether customer receipts arrive in time.

The final choice is not “borrow or never borrow.” It is whether the financing instrument, payment schedule and fallback fit the asset or cash cycle and the owner's tolerance for risk. If the downside path breaches reserve and there is no committed contingency, reduce the scale or wait. If the project remains cash-positive after realistic delays and maintenance, borrowing can fund a return that exceeds its cost. Record the assumptions and decision date so the owner can revisit them when actual performance replaces the forecast.


## A worked reserve and distribution decision

Imagine the business has KSh 2.4 million in unrestricted cash and no other confirmed inflow for four weeks. Payroll is KSh 420,000, rent KSh 160,000, tax remittance KSh 180,000, critical supplier invoices KSh 560,000 and debt service KSh 140,000. A planned machine overhaul requires a KSh 300,000 deposit in week three. Those obligations total KSh 1.76 million, leaving KSh 640,000 before reserve. The owner has set a KSh 450,000 reserve based on a two-week payroll and essential-supplier response window, leaving a theoretical KSh 190,000 above reserve.

Now test the largest buyer paying KSh 500,000 three weeks late. If that receipt was not included in the KSh 2.4 million cash already on hand, the owner must not subtract it from today's cash; instead it delays replenishment and changes the next forecast. Suppose the delay also means an extra KSh 250,000 of stock must be purchased for prepaid orders. The trough becomes KSh 390,000, below the chosen reserve by KSh 60,000. A KSh 190,000 distribution is no longer safe. The owner could defer the payment to themselves, ask for a buyer deposit, negotiate staged supplier delivery or use a committed revolver. The model should show each option’s timing, interest cost and service effect.

This calculation separates three decisions. The workshop may have earned enough annual owner earnings to support a distribution over time. It may have KSh 190,000 of apparent liquidity on the base schedule. But under one credible collection delay and order requirement, the same distribution would breach the reserve. A prudent decision can approve a smaller amount now and revisit after the buyer’s payment clears. The reserve level remains a management choice and should be revisited if payroll, seasonality or facility access changes.

## Read a loan request as a transaction, not a slogan

When a lender asks “how much do you need?”, answer with the cash bridge. List the invoice or asset being funded, own contribution, supplier advance, delivery date, customer acceptance date, invoice terms, expected receipt and fallback. If the funding need is general, divide it by use: inventory, payroll, tax arrears, equipment, refinancing or expansion. Some uses may be appropriate for a revolving facility, others for a term loan, and some may indicate a need for equity or restructuring.

A lender can test whether the borrower has a repayment source. The borrower should test whether the repayment schedule matches actual collections and asset life. Compare the debt draw and repayment dates to the 13-week cash forecast, then extend the forecast through the final maturity. Stress a rate reset, fee, delayed customer and shorter supplier terms. Show cash after debt service and after maintenance, not just EBITDA divided by annual interest. If the facility is secured by a vehicle or property, show whether losing that asset would interrupt the very sales forecast used to repay the debt.

Do not borrow solely because a limit is available. A facility with an unused fee may still provide useful resilience, but the owner should know how quickly it can be drawn and what conditions must be met. Conversely, avoiding all debt can cause the business to miss a profitable order even when collections are well supported. Compare expected incremental return with total financing cost and downside loss. The decision note should identify what evidence would cause the owner to cancel the draw.

## Put accountability around the policy

Assign the owner or finance lead to update cash weekly, the bookkeeper to reconcile monthly, and the board or co-owners to approve distributions above the agreed cap. If one person controls all cash, statements, supplier terms and records, a trusted backup should be able to verify the balances during absence. Maintain dual authorization for large transfers if practical. Strong access controls protect business continuity as well as against fraud.

Save the policy alongside each forecast. A cash reserve rule is only credible if management can point to why it exists, what obligations it covers, and how it will be reviewed. Record exceptions, who approved them and how the business replenished the reserve. If the owner repeatedly overrides the policy for personal drawings, revisit the amount or the household cash plan rather than pretending the exceptions never happened.

The owner’s pay, the lender’s repayment and the growth project all draw on the same operating cash. Put them in one dated forecast, then assess each against the business’s ability to continue trading, maintain service and meet contracts. That is how an owner can make a practical choice without confusing a promising return with cash already earned.

## Decide what each shilling of cash is for

A business owner should separate four decisions that are often mixed together: pay for work performed, repayment of money previously lent by the owner, distribution of accumulated profit, and reinvestment in the company. Use distinct ledger descriptions, approvals and tax treatment. A cash withdrawal is not automatically a dividend, and a dividend is not a substitute for a reasonable salary where the owner works in the business.

Set a distribution calendar, but make the payment conditional on the cash forecast and legal requirements. At each review, ask whether the company has paid or reserved for payroll, taxes, debt service, critical suppliers, committed maintenance and the next working-capital peak. Only then consider discretionary owner distributions. This avoids promising a fixed amount in a month when cash is tied up in a large customer order or seasonal stock build.

A reserve policy should identify the risk it covers, how much cash is assigned, where it is held, who can release it and when the policy is reviewed. Separate an operating buffer for timing mismatches from a specific repair reserve, tax reserve or debt-service reserve. Do not count the same bank balance as backing multiple purposes. For a business with seasonal inflows, show the low point over the full operating cycle; the annual average can hide the month when the company runs short.

## Compare growth projects with a common test

An attractive growth idea is not self-funding just because the projected margin is positive. Estimate the initial equipment and setup cost, working capital, hiring and training, launch period, ongoing maintenance, taxes, customer-acquisition cost and time to collect. Identify the minimum volume needed to cover incremental fixed cost and the downside point at which the project consumes cash without a realistic recovery path.

A simple investment page might show three cases:

* **Base case:** demand supported by signed orders, repeat purchases or a documented pipeline; realistic collection days; full staff and service costs.
* **Downside case:** slower launch, lower utilization, a price concession, late customer payment, or a longer equipment installation.
* **Stop case:** the condition that prevents further spending, such as a missed permit, customer cancellation, cost overrun, or failure to reach a milestone by an agreed date.

Ask what the business will stop doing if this project receives cash. Capital has an opportunity cost even when the owner uses retained profits rather than a bank loan. A project that is strategically appealing can still be sequenced later if it would leave no money for current maintenance or payroll.

## Borrow for a defined cash gap, with a repayment source

Debt can be sensible when it funds an asset or temporary working-capital need that produces cash before repayment falls due. It is dangerous when a short-term facility is used to cover recurring losses or a permanent mismatch between customer terms and supplier terms. Before borrowing, write down the amount and date of the gap, the use of proceeds, the expected cash source for each repayment, and the consequence if that source arrives late.

Compare the full cost, not only the quoted rate: arrangement and commitment fees, insurance, required balances, security costs, legal charges, early repayment terms, collateral, guarantees, maturity and renewal risk. Compare a term loan matched to an asset's useful cash-generating life with a revolving facility for a genuine short-cycle need. The best structure depends on cash timing and contract terms, not a general claim that one product is always cheaper.

Credit rationing research explains why lenders may limit credit when they cannot distinguish borrower quality or fully observe risk [2]. The practical response is to make the company's own record easier to evaluate: current management accounts, reconciled bank statements, aged receivables, stock evidence, tax status, existing debt schedule, forecast, facility purpose and downside repayment plan. A lender pack should disclose risks and sensitivities, not just present an optimistic case.

## Review whether the decision worked

For every material distribution, loan or growth decision, record the forecast at approval and compare it with actual results after 30, 90 and 180 days. Track cash generated, margin, collection time, service failures, covenant headroom and the assumptions that were wrong. If the owner repeatedly draws more than normalized free cash, decide whether that reflects a temporary investment phase, a weak cash conversion cycle, an underpriced product, or a business that cannot support the owner's required income. The answer changes whether the next step is improved collections, repricing, cost redesign, a smaller distribution or a planned exit.
