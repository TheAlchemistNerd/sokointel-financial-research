# Part 01 — The cash an owner can actually claim

*Revised practical article; source details and figures are dated to their cited filing or event, with current evidence reviewed through 24 September 2026.*

An owner opens the mobile banking app and sees KSh 1.2 million. How much of it can they take home?

The tempting answer is “the cash balance.” The careful answer begins with a different question: what does that cash have to do before it belongs to the owner? Payroll is due. A customer has paid in advance for goods the business has not delivered. A tax instalment is scheduled. A loan repayment falls next week. The business needs stock before the next sales cycle. The account balance is real, but it has several claimants.

That is why this series treats “owner earnings” as a way of thinking about earning power, not as a synonym for cash, profit, or a dividend. Buffett’s 1986 shareholder letter described owner earnings as reported earnings plus certain non-cash charges, less the average capital expenditure needed to maintain long-term competitive position and unit volume, and less additional working capital needed for the same purpose. He also warned that the maintenance-capital estimate can be difficult [1]. That caveat belongs in the formula, not in a footnote.

## Four questions, four answers

Start by separating four measures:

1. **Cash generated in the period** asks what entered and left the bank accounts for one stated period.
2. **Accounting profit** matches recognized income and expenses under the business’s accounting basis. It can differ from cash because customers may owe money, stock may remain unsold, or invoices may be unpaid.
3. **Owner earnings** estimates how much the business can earn over time after reinvestment needed to keep the operation competitive and at the same level of activity.
4. **Cash that can prudently be distributed** asks what can leave the business after upcoming obligations, debt principal, restrictions, and a sensible operating reserve.

They may happen to be close in a stable, simple business. They often diverge in a growing or seasonal one. A fast-growing shop may report a profit while cash is tied up in new inventory and unpaid invoices. A business may also show cash because it drew a loan. That cash came with a matching liability. It is not sales revenue.

A useful starting bridge for an owner-managed business is:

> Reported profit after a market-level payment for the owner’s work  
> + relevant non-cash charges, if they have not already been included in the starting cash measure  
> − estimated maintenance and replacement investment  
> − additional working capital needed for the activity being sustained  
> = an owner-earnings estimate

Then run a separate liquidity bridge:

> Cash available before distribution  
> − due payroll, supplier and tax payments  
> − scheduled loan principal and other required cash commitments  
> − required or chosen operating reserve  
> = cash the owner could consider distributing

If the same expense appears in both bridges, check for double-counting. For instance, do not begin with net income, subtract an expense again, and call that conservatism. State the treatment once.

## A small example

Imagine a repair shop with one owner who also manages the staff and works at the counter. For this fictional year, after recording a reasonable wage for that work, the books show KSh 800,000 of profit. Depreciation is KSh 150,000. The business has bought replacement tools, but it also opened a second service bay. The accounts do not identify which shillings of equipment spending merely kept the first bay running and which created extra capacity.

A model might show a maintenance range of KSh 120,000–250,000, with a central working assumption of KSh 180,000. If maintaining the existing volume also ties up an extra KSh 100,000 in parts and customer credit, the central estimate is:

`KSh 800,000 + 150,000 − 180,000 − 100,000 = KSh 670,000`

That is an estimate of owner earnings under those assumptions. It is not a precise audited line. The book’s cash could still be lower because old invoices remain unpaid, a loan principal payment is due, or a tax payment differs from the tax expense recognized during the year. Nor should the owner automatically withdraw KSh 670,000. First reconcile the actual bank movement and list obligations due before the next reliable receipts.

The owner’s labour matters too. If the owner worked full-time but recorded no salary, the apparent profit includes pay for a job. A buyer would need someone to do that work. For valuation, estimate the cost of competent replacement labour and subtract it before treating the remaining profit as a return on ownership. For a sole proprietor deciding what to transfer to a personal account, salary and owner draw may be booked differently. The economic question is still: did the business earn a return after paying for the work that keeps it open?

## What the research adds

Working-capital decisions can shape both profit and available cash. Deloof studied 1,009 large Belgian non-financial firms and found associations between profitability and the time firms held receivables and inventory [2]. García-Teruel and Martínez-Solano examined 8,872 Spanish SMEs and reported that shortening collection periods and inventory holding periods was associated with higher profitability in their sample [3]. Those are useful reasons to measure the cash cycle. They are not a target that every Kenyan shop should hit. A wholesaler who cuts stock below what reliable service requires may lose customers. A small supplier who shortens payment terms may lose a large buyer.

For a lender or a buyer, the entity also matters. A loan in the owner’s personal name, a company overdraft, a director’s guarantee, and a supplier balance may all affect risk, but they are not the same liability. The Business Credit series already demonstrates why a lender’s legal borrower and the business whose cash flow is being assessed must be joined carefully. The cash is only available to the party that owns the account and is free to use it under its contracts. Merton’s structural credit model is a useful reminder that equity and debt are different claims on the same business assets [4]. It is not a substitute for the owner’s dated cash schedule: the model needs asset value and risk inputs that most small businesses do not observe reliably.

## A five-minute owner check

Before taking money out, the owner can write down:

* bank cash today, including which entity owns each account;
* receipts that are actually expected, with dates and evidence;
* payments due before those receipts arrive;
* stock or equipment the business must buy to deliver existing commitments;
* debt principal, tax and payroll due;
* minimum cash needed to survive a delayed customer payment or a weak sales week.

This small schedule is more useful than calling every available shilling “profit.” A 13-week forecast appears in Part 3. The next part looks at the most judgmental line in the owner-earnings estimate: how much investment it really takes to maintain the business.


## Rebuild the year before asking what can be drawn

Take the last twelve months and put the business cash story in one schedule. Reconcile sales to invoices, till receipts, mobile payments and merchant settlements. A KSh 2 million sale recorded on credit is not KSh 2 million in the bank. Reconcile purchases and payroll to invoices, payslips and payment runs. A bill received after year end may belong to the period even if it has not been paid. The point is to know which timing differences explain cash, not to force cash to equal profit.

Use a repeatable order:

1. **Choose one legal entity and period.** If the shop, warehouse and vehicle are owned by different companies or relatives, identify intercompany rent, loans, guarantees and assets before combining cash.
2. **Tie opening and closing cash to statements.** Include bank, mobile money and till cash separately. Remove restricted deposits and amounts held for tax or customers.
3. **Tie revenue and costs to records.** Identify VAT or other indirect tax, refunds, discounts, bad debt, stock adjustments and personal spending.
4. **Reconcile operating cash.** Add back noncash charges once, then reflect actual changes in receivables, stock, supplier balances and other operating items.
5. **Classify investment.** List cash asset additions and disposals; distinguish maintenance, compliance, replacement and growth using asset-level evidence.
6. **Separate financing.** Draws are receipts from financing; principal repayments are cash outflows. Interest treatment depends on the starting point and valuation question.
7. **Normalize owner work and one-offs.** Replace below-market or above-market compensation with supportable costs for necessary work.
8. **State working-capital assumptions.** Determine whether balances reflect normal trading, seasonality, overdue suppliers or one-time catch-up.
9. **Calculate separate answers for earning power and liquidity.** Owner earnings is not the same as cash available this week.

This is more demanding than “profit plus depreciation,” but it reveals missing books. If the owner cannot reconcile deposits to sales, improve transaction capture before building a more complicated valuation.

## A detailed fictional cash bridge

Suppose a workshop reports KSh 1.8 million net income after paying the proprietor KSh 900,000 for management and repair work. Depreciation is KSh 240,000. Receivables rise from KSh 800,000 to KSh 1.05 million, inventory from KSh 1.20 million to KSh 1.45 million, and trade payables from KSh 700,000 to KSh 830,000. On these simplified balances, incremental net working capital consumes KSh 370,000: 250,000 more receivables plus 250,000 more stock less 130,000 more payables. Cash from operations before other accruals is roughly KSh 1.67 million.

The shop spends KSh 680,000 on equipment. The asset list shows KSh 380,000 replaces two lifts and a diagnostic unit supporting current service; KSh 300,000 fits out a second bay. A simple historical cash figure after all capex is KSh 990,000. Yet that is not normalized earning power: actual capex includes growth, while future maintenance may not equal this year’s replacement spend.

For an owner-earnings view, the KSh 1.8 million profit already includes KSh 900,000 compensation. A qualified replacement manager and mechanic would cost KSh 1.2 million. Adjust down by only the KSh 300,000 shortfall; do not subtract the full replacement wage again. Using KSh 380,000 maintenance and KSh 370,000 additional working capital, normalized owner earnings are **KSh 990,000**, calculated as KSh 1.8 million profit + KSh 240,000 depreciation − KSh 380,000 maintenance − KSh 370,000 additional working capital − KSh 300,000 replacement-pay gap. The KSh 1.29 million figure is the intermediate estimate before the replacement-pay adjustment; it is not the final normalized owner-earnings figure. This bridge also happens to equal the KSh 990,000 historical cash-after-total-capex proxy because this example's KSh 300,000 growth-bay outlay and KSh 300,000 replacement-pay adjustment offset numerically. That coincidence will not generally hold. The KSh 300,000 growth bay remains a separate investment decision, not maintenance; test its expected incremental return separately and do not deduct it again from the no-growth owner-earnings estimate.

The distribution question uses actual dates. If the bank has KSh 1.2 million, upcoming payroll is KSh 250,000, critical suppliers KSh 260,000, rent and utilities KSh 80,000, taxes KSh 90,000, loan principal KSh 100,000, and a prepaid order requires KSh 130,000 to complete, those uses total KSh 910,000. With a KSh 150,000 reserve floor, only KSh 140,000 is provisionally above the listed needs. If a disputed KSh 200,000 customer receipt was mistakenly counted, even that headroom disappears. A business can earn KSh 1.29 million over a normalized year and still have little safe cash for an immediate draw.

Every line must be tested. Check maintenance with the technician and quotations; replacement cost with the actual job description; working capital across several seasons; and the forecast with dated receipts. Keep the bridge auditable enough that a lender, partner or buyer can challenge it.

## Reconcile annual cash to the bank statement

For each month, use the identity: opening unrestricted cash plus operating, financing and asset-sale receipts, less operating payments, capex, financing payments and owner distributions equals closing unrestricted cash. Tie the result to bank and mobile-money reconciliations after transfers between accounts are removed. If it does not tie, list the difference and investigate instead of hiding it in “other.”

A facility draw is financing, not sales cash; principal repayment is not an operating expense. Reconcile tax payable, tax expense and tax paid rather than subtracting the same tax twice. Customer deposits are receipts with delivery or refund obligations. Supplier credit can increase cash temporarily but is not sustainable working capital if it reflects overdue balances. The cash schedule should show each item on its due date.

Separate **cash control** from **profit measurement**. A bank reconciliation shows that cash exists, not that profit recurs. A stocktake checks quantity, not saleability. An invoice confirms a ledger entry, not collection. A monthly close should test both the existence and economic quality of the asset or income.

## Use a monthly owner review

A monthly review can fit on one page: reported profit; profit-to-cash bridge; maintenance estimate and range; incremental working-capital use or release; market replacement pay; scheduled debt; reserve requirement; and distributions already taken. Beside each figure, record source and owner. Mark it tied, estimated or unresolved. If an unresolved line changes a drawing, loan or hiring decision, resolve it before acting.

Compare the forecast with actual dates and amounts. If collections repeatedly arrive ten days late, use observed timing instead of the sales team's best-case promise. If a repair was larger because replacement had been deferred, update the asset plan. If the owner withdrew money for a personal emergency, record the draw separately and restore the agreed reserve process. This prevents a one-off event from being misreported as ordinary performance.

The practical sequence is: close the books, reconcile cash, estimate sustainable earnings, protect the reserve, then approve a transfer. Use the earnings estimate to assess durable returns, the cash plan for today's payment decision, and a valuation method only after ownership and financing claims are reconciled.


## Three ways the bridge can mislead

The first is double counting. If the starting figure is net income, tax expense and interest expense have already affected it. If the analysis adds back interest to estimate enterprise cash flow, debt service still belongs in the later financing bridge. If it starts from cash flow after interest and then subtracts interest again, it understates cash. Write the basis at the top of the schedule: equity earnings after interest, operating cash before financing, or cash available for distribution. Every add-back should have a clear reason and a matching treatment elsewhere.

The second is treating all noncash expense as disposable cash. Depreciation is not a current payment, but the assets that depreciate wear out. Adding back depreciation without a replacement estimate turns an income statement convention into a claim that equipment is free. Amortization may relate to software, licenses or acquired customer relationships with different replacement economics. Add back only where appropriate to the chosen measure, and then explicitly fund the renewal or replacement need.

The third is interpreting working-capital release as recurring earnings. A business that collects old receivables or reduces stock may generate cash this year. It cannot repeat the same release indefinitely. If the normalized closing balance is lower than the opening balance because the firm liquidated excess stock, separate that one-time release from the annual owner-earnings estimate. If supplier balances rose because invoices were unpaid, do not treat that as permanent free financing.

## Adapt the claim to the business

A bakery’s cash bridge should include flour and packaging bought before daily sales, equipment replacement, waste and retailer settlement timing. A professional services firm may have little physical inventory but can carry large receivables, unbilled work, software subscriptions and key-person exposure. A farm can have substantial seasonal inventory and biological production cycles; profit and cash receipt dates can fall in different periods. A small property developer may receive deposits that are tied to units still to be completed. The same four questions apply, but the working-capital and maintenance lines are different.

For a service business, the replacement-capital question may be software, cybersecurity, employee training, regulatory licenses and customer acquisition needed to keep retention stable. For a vehicle operator, it includes tyres, engines, permits and downtime. For a retailer, it includes shelving and point-of-sale systems as well as stock availability. This is why an analyst should ask the people who operate the assets what spending preserves current service before setting the owner-earnings line.

The owner can start with three columns: reported, adjusted and unresolved. Put source values in reported. Put only evidenced corrections in adjusted. Put estimates and missing records in unresolved with a low/base/high range when possible. This keeps the reader from mistaking a plausible assumption for an audited fact. The best bridge is not the one with the fewest open items; it is the one that tells management which unresolved item could change the decision.

## Decide whether a draw is repeatable

A draw can be funded by four different things: current operating cash, accumulated cash from prior good years, new borrowing, or selling an asset. Only the first may indicate current earning capacity, and even then the business must fund obligations and replacement needs. The owner should label each source. A special distribution funded from an old cash reserve might be entirely reasonable, but it should not become a monthly expectation that forces the company to borrow later.

Before a regular owner draw, compare the proposed annual amount with normalized owner earnings over several periods and with downside liquidity. If owner earnings vary sharply by season, set a lower regular amount and make a later year-end distribution only after the peak cash need has passed. If the business is expanding, decide whether the growth return exceeds the owner’s alternative use of cash. If the business is not earning enough after replacement labor and maintenance, a large transfer can turn an accounting profit into a weaker business.

The decision note can be short: “The business earned an estimated KSh X after replacement labor and maintenance under the base case; the low case is KSh Y. The next 13 weeks remain above reserve only if customer A pays by date D. We approve a draw of KSh Z today and will revisit after the cash is received.” That sentence states the estimate, condition, amount and review point. It is much more useful than saying the bank balance looks healthy.

## Turn the bridge into a monthly decision

The annual owner-earnings bridge is useful only if the owner can reproduce it from the books and the bank. Build the same bridge monthly, then roll it forward over twelve months. For each month, show cash collected from customers, cash paid to suppliers and employees, tax paid, routine capital spending, principal and interest, and owner withdrawals. Reconcile the ending cash to the bank statement. This gives the owner two answers: what the business earned over a representative period and whether that earning has actually arrived as spendable cash.

Suppose a distributor reports KSh 900,000 of monthly accounting profit. Its owner performs purchasing and collections work worth KSh 160,000 a month at a realistic replacement cost. The business has KSh 140,000 of routine equipment and vehicle renewal, and receivables and stock have absorbed KSh 240,000 more cash than the prior month. Before debt principal, tax timing and reserve decisions, the month has not produced KSh 900,000 of owner cash. The example is not a universal formula or margin benchmark; it shows why each adjustment needs its own line and evidence.

The sequence matters. Deduct a replacement wage before treating owner labour as an add-back. Deduct normal maintenance investment after examining the actual assets and their service condition. Show working-capital movements as a cash timing item, not a permanent annual charge: a build-up consumes cash, while a release can temporarily inflate it. Then show taxes, interest and principal according to the purpose of the measure. A lender's cash-flow definition may use a different sequence from a shareholder's residual cash measure.

The monthly close should make timing visible. A December profit supported by invoices collected in February is profit, but it does not fund a December supplier payment unless credit is available. Keep the income statement, cash-flow movement and balance-sheet roll-forward side by side. The business owner can then distinguish an earnings problem from a collections problem and a temporary seasonal draw from a recurring structural deficit. Research on working capital and SME performance emphasizes that its cash requirements and its effect on performance are linked, but the relationship is not a one-direction rule that says less working capital is always better [2, 3].

## Use a short forecast to test whether the cash is safe to take

A distribution decision should begin with a rolling 13-week cash forecast. Start with reconciled opening bank cash and show weekly collections by named customer or customer group, payroll, critical suppliers, tax, rent, debt service, capex and owner withdrawals. The base case should use expected payment dates grounded in collection history, not invoice due dates alone. A downside case can move the largest collections later, reduce sales where orders are not yet secured, and include known repairs or tax obligations.

The lowest projected weekly balance is more decision-useful than the year-end balance. If the forecast falls below the operating floor, owner withdrawals should wait or be funded through a documented financing plan. Set the floor from obligations and recovery time: payroll and statutory payments, the cash needed to continue essential deliveries, the likely delay before emergency funding, and the cost of restarting after a stock-out or service interruption. Do not copy a rule such as “three months of expenses” without checking seasonality and the actual commitments.

The forecast should be refreshed every week. At the review, compare forecast against actual cash and explain the three biggest differences. If collections repeatedly arrive later than forecast, change the assumptions and collection process. If a supplier asks for a deposit earlier than expected, confirm the purchase and delivery before moving the date. The forecast is an operating tool; it should change as evidence changes.

## Keep a distribution ledger

A distribution ledger records date, amount, purpose, approving person, forecast headroom, remaining reserves and any tax or legal treatment. Separate wages for work, repayment of a documented shareholder loan, dividends or profit distributions, and personal expenses paid by the business. These categories have different accounting, tax, governance and solvency implications. The ledger prevents a strong month from obscuring repeated owner draws that were never compared with reinvestment needs.

At quarter-end, compare total distributions with normalized earnings and actual free cash over a rolling period. If cash paid out is persistently greater than cash produced after maintenance and normal working capital, the difference must come from borrowing, asset sales, a working-capital release, or delayed obligations. Name the source. This is the practical meaning of the owner-earnings question: not merely what could be withdrawn in theory, but what can be taken without weakening the operation that produces next year's earnings.
