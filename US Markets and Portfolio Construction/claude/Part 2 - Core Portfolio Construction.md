# US Markets and Portfolio Construction
## Part 2 of 5: Core Portfolio Construction — Strategic Allocation and the Core, Core-Plus, and Core-Satellite Frameworks

Part 1 established the vocabulary: which indices exist, which vehicles and legal structures deliver them, and what each layer of cost actually costs. None of that, on its own, tells an investor how much to hold in any one fund. This part turns the building blocks into a deliberate structure, using three related frameworks — core, core-plus, and core-satellite — that describe, in increasing order of complexity, how a portfolio can be organized around a dominant, low-cost foundation. Each framework answers the same underlying question with a different amount of flexibility: how much of the portfolio should be committed to broad, diversified, low-cost exposure before any room is left for a specific view, a tilt, or a conviction position, and how tightly should that remaining room be bounded so it cannot quietly grow into something the investor never actually decided to hold.

### Why the foundation matters more than the details

A widely cited finding from institutional investing research is a useful starting point, provided it is stated carefully. Brinson, Hood, and Beebower's 1986 study of large US pension plans found that a plan's strategic asset-mix policy explained, on average, 93.6% of the variance in that plan's own quarterly returns over time [1]. A follow-up study by Brinson, Singer, and Beebower in 1991 found a similar figure of 91.5% [2]. What this actually shows is that a single plan's period-to-period ups and downs were overwhelmingly a function of its policy mix between stocks, bonds, and cash, rather than the timing or security-selection decisions layered on top of it — a time-series finding about one portfolio's fluctuation, not a cross-sectional claim that policy explains 93.6% of why one investor ends up richer than another. Subsequent commentary, including CFA Institute's own review of the debate, has stressed this distinction precisely because the looser, more sweeping version of the finding spread far further than the careful original result [3]. The defensible takeaway is narrower than the popular one, but it still points the same direction: the broad policy mix an investor commits to is doing most of the work in determining how a portfolio behaves over time, which is exactly why this part starts with that mix rather than with individual security or fund selection.

The critique is worth a moment longer, because it changes what an investor should actually conclude from the finding. The 93.6% figure is a time-series R-squared: it explains how much of a single plan's quarter-to-quarter wobble is attributable to its policy mix, holding that plan's own manager-selection and timing decisions constant. It does not directly answer the separate, arguably more practically important question of why one investor ends up with a higher or lower total return than another investor over the same period — a cross-sectional question, for which subsequent researchers have found policy mix explains a much smaller share of the variation, since two investors with similar policy mixes can still diverge meaningfully in outcome through fund selection, timing, and cost drag. Ibbotson and Kaplan's year-2000 update reached a similar time-series conclusion using mutual fund rather than pension data, which suggests the original finding was not an artifact of the specific pension-plan sample, but it does not resolve the cross-sectional question either. None of this weakens the practical case for deciding on a policy mix deliberately; it only means the case rests on "policy mix is the dominant driver of a given portfolio's period-to-period behavior," which is well supported, rather than on the looser and more commonly repeated claim that policy mix alone explains why some investors do better than others, which the original study was never designed to test.

Vanguard's own guide to core-satellite construction frames the practical implication directly: a portfolio's strategic policy — its target mix of core exposures — should be set deliberately and held with discipline, while any more tactical or thematic conviction an investor wants to express should be sized so that it cannot overwhelm that policy even if it turns out to be wrong [1]. That framing is the organizing idea for the rest of this part.

### Defining "core"

A core holding, in this framework, is one that is broad, low-cost, and low-turnover, tracking a well-diversified benchmark rather than a narrow theme. Part 1 introduced four candidate US equity core benchmarks — the S&P 500, the Nasdaq-100, the Dow Jones Industrial Average, and the Russell family — and it is worth being explicit about which of these actually functions well as a core holding and which does not. The Dow's price-weighted, committee-selected, 30-stock construction makes it a poor core choice on its own: it is not diversified across the market by any rules-based sizing methodology, and its price-weighting means a handful of high-priced constituents can dominate its movement regardless of company size. The Nasdaq-100's exclusion of an entire sector — financials — makes it, by design, an incomplete cross-section of the US economy; it can be a valid core-plus or satellite tilt, discussed below, but a portfolio holding only the Nasdaq-100 is not holding a diversified core.

The S&P 500 and the broader Russell 3000 (or an equivalent total-market fund) are the more defensible core candidates. The S&P 500's Investable Weight Factor methodology gives a float-adjusted, market-cap-weighted cross-section of roughly 500 large US companies across all eleven GICS sectors, while the Russell 3000 extends that cross-section down through mid- and small-cap companies to represent close to 98% of investable US equity value [4]. An investor choosing between the two is really deciding how much small-cap exposure they want baked into the "core" versus added deliberately later as a tilt — both are legitimate starting points, and this series uses the S&P 500 as its working example throughout, consistent with the vehicles already introduced in Part 1, while noting that a total-market fund is an equally valid substitute for a reader who prefers broader small-cap inclusion at the core level. The choice is not purely cosmetic: small-cap stocks have, over some historical periods, earned a return premium over large-cap stocks that some factor-investing research attributes to their higher risk and lower liquidity, though the size of that premium — and whether it persists going forward at all — remains genuinely disputed among researchers, with extended historical stretches in which small caps trailed large caps for a decade or more. An S&P-500-only core simply omits this debate by construction; a total-market core takes a small, permanent position on one side of it. Neither choice needs to be justified by a confident forecast about which will win going forward, since both remain diversified, broad-based core holdings by the definition used throughout this part — the difference is one of degree, not of category.

It is worth being equally explicit about what disqualifies a holding from serving as a core position at all, since the label gets applied loosely in casual investing discussion. A single stock, however large or well established the company, is not a core holding under this definition, because its return depends on the fortunes of one management team and one business model rather than a diversified cross-section of them; it belongs in a satellite sleeve, sized accordingly, discussed in Part 3. A leveraged or inverse fund — one designed to deliver two or three times a benchmark's daily return, or its inverse — is not a core holding either, and not merely because of its added volatility: these products are built to compound their stated multiple over a single trading day, and their return over any longer holding period can diverge substantially from a simple multiple of the index's return, a mechanical effect of daily compounding known as volatility decay that makes them structurally unsuited to a long-term core allocation regardless of an investor's conviction in the underlying benchmark's direction. A narrow sector or thematic fund is not a core holding for the same essential reason a single stock is not: it concentrates return in one segment of the economy rather than spreading it broadly, however diversified its individual holdings might be within that segment.

### Choosing among S&P 500 vehicles, revisited

Part 1's Table 1 already showed that SPYM, VOO, IVV, and SPY track functionally the same S&P 500 index at expense ratios of 0.02%, 0.03%, 0.03%, and 0.0945% respectively [5], [6], [7]. That comparison bears directly on core selection: because the four vehicles are close substitutes for the same underlying exposure, the core decision at the vehicle level reduces mostly to cost, liquidity, and brokerage-specific considerations such as commission-free trading lists, rather than to any meaningful difference in what is actually owned. This series uses SPYM as its running example for the S&P 500 sleeve of a core allocation on cost grounds alone, while noting that VOO and IVV are functionally interchangeable substitutes for an investor whose broker does not offer SPYM commission-free.

### Adding a bond sleeve to the core

A core built entirely from US equities is not yet a complete strategic policy; it is missing an asset class whose return pattern differs enough from equities to change the shape of the portfolio's ups and downs, not just its average return. The two largest, lowest-cost vehicles for broad US investment-grade bond exposure are Vanguard's BND, tracking the float-adjusted Bloomberg US Aggregate Index at a 0.03% expense ratio, and iShares' AGG, tracking the unadjusted version of the same benchmark, also at 0.03% [8], [9]. Both hold a mix of Treasuries, agency mortgage-backed securities, and investment-grade corporate debt across the maturity spectrum, and both function as a "core" bond holding in the same sense that VOO or SPYM function as a core equity holding: broad, cheap, and representative of the asset class as a whole rather than a narrow slice of it.

A broad aggregate-bond core is not free of its own risks, and it is worth naming them rather than treating "bonds" as a single undifferentiated ballast asset. Both BND and AGG carry meaningful interest-rate (duration) risk: because bond prices move inversely to yields, a broad-based aggregate bond fund with an effective duration in the middle of the maturity spectrum will still lose value in a rising-rate environment, as both funds' own reported multi-year drawdowns during the 2022 rate-hiking cycle illustrate. An aggregate bond core also carries no explicit inflation protection; an investor specifically concerned with inflation risk, as distinct from general rate risk, would look to Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities (TIPS) funds as a further refinement rather than assume the aggregate-bond core already covers that risk. None of this argues against holding a bond sleeve — it argues for understanding what kind of risk it does and does not diversify away, which is precisely the question Equation (2.1) below is built to make concrete.

### Core-plus: modest, bounded tilts within the same broad structure

A core-plus approach keeps the bulk of the portfolio in the broad core described above but permits modest, deliberate tilts adjacent to it — overweighting growth relative to the broad market, for instance, or adding a quality or dividend-growth screen — without introducing an entirely separate, narrowly concentrated position. Adding a Nasdaq-100 allocation such as QQQM, at a 0.15% expense ratio [10], alongside a broad S&P 500 core is a clean example: it tilts the portfolio's growth and technology exposure upward relative to the market-cap-neutral S&P 500 baseline, but because the Nasdaq-100 still holds roughly 100 large, liquid companies rather than a handful of thematically linked names, the tilt remains diversified even as it expresses a view. The defining feature of core-plus, in other words, is that the "plus" is still built from broad, rules-based indices — it changes the portfolio's factor exposure without concentrating it in a small number of names or a narrow theme.

Other tilts fit the same pattern without necessarily involving the Nasdaq-100 at all. A value tilt, achieved through a broad value-index fund rather than a handful of individually selected "cheap" stocks, shifts the portfolio's factor exposure toward companies trading at lower valuation multiples relative to fundamentals, a long-studied factor whose premium has nonetheless varied considerably by decade and shows extended periods of underperformance alongside its long-run average. An international-equity tilt — allocating a deliberate slice of the equity sleeve to a broad ex-US developed- or emerging-market index fund rather than concentrating entirely in US-domiciled companies — is another common core-plus decision, and arguably the most consequential one this series does not resolve on the reader's behalf, since "US Markets and Portfolio Construction" is, by its stated scope, a series about building blocks available on US exchanges rather than a argument for or against home-country bias. An investor following this series to build a genuinely globally diversified portfolio should treat the US-only examples used throughout as one deliberately scoped illustration, not as an implicit recommendation to exclude non-US equities altogether.

### Core-satellite: a majority core plus distinct, bounded conviction sleeves

Core-satellite construction goes a step further, adding one or more genuinely narrow, high-conviction positions — a single sector, a specific investment theme, or an income-generating options strategy — deliberately bounded in size so that even a poor outcome in the satellite sleeve cannot derail the portfolio's overall policy. Part 3 of this series develops satellite construction in full, using a semiconductor-sector case study and a dividend-focused sleeve such as Schwab's SCHD, currently priced at a 0.06% expense ratio and built around a rules-based screen for dividend-payment history, cash-flow-to-debt, return on equity, dividend yield, and dividend growth [11]. What matters for this part is the sizing discipline itself: a satellite sleeve is, by construction, less diversified than the core it sits alongside, and the whole premise of core-satellite investing collapses if the satellite is allowed to grow, through a combination of new contributions and its own outperformance, into a large enough share of the portfolio that its concentration risk becomes the portfolio's concentration risk. Guarding against that drift — sometimes called satellite creep — is a matter of setting a maximum satellite weight in advance and rebalancing back to it on a set schedule or when it is breached by a set threshold, a topic Part 5 covers in full alongside the mechanics of rebalancing more generally.

The blended expense ratio of 0.067% costs this illustrative portfolio about $33.62 per year at current asset values — a useful sanity check figure, since a portfolio built entirely from broad, low-cost vehicles should produce a blended expense ratio well under a tenth of one percent even after including two satellite sleeves priced above the core. ### The same framework, two different risk postures

The core-satellite structure in Table 2 is one point on a spectrum, not the only correct answer, and it is worth showing how the same five building blocks can be re-weighted for a different investor without changing the underlying logic. A more conservative variant — appropriate for an investor with a shorter horizon or lower tolerance for drawdown — might shift the bond core up to 45% and trim the equity core, growth tilt, and satellites accordingly:

| Sleeve | Conservative variant | Growth-oriented variant |
|---|---|---|
| SPYM (S&P 500 core) | 35.0% | 55.0% |
| AGG (bond core) | 45.0% | 5.0% |
| QQQM (growth tilt) | 5.0% | 20.0% |
| Dividend-quality satellite | 10.0% | 5.0% |
| Sector satellite | 5.0% | 15.0% |
| **Blended expense ratio** | **0.0505%** | **0.0950%** |

Both variants keep the combined satellite allocation at or under 20% and hold every position through the same five broad vehicles introduced earlier in this part; what changes is only the relative weight given to the ballast (the bond core) versus the growth-seeking sleeves (the Nasdaq-100 tilt and the satellites), which is precisely the kind of adjustment an investor's own time horizon and risk tolerance should drive. Note, too, that the blended expense ratio nearly doubles between the two variants — not because either individual fund became more expensive, but because the growth-oriented variant simply holds more of its assets in the higher-cost QQQM and sector-satellite sleeves relative to the near-free bond and S&P 500 core. Cost comparisons across different portfolios are only meaningful once the underlying allocation is held fixed; comparing two portfolios' blended expense ratios without accounting for what each is actually invested in can be as misleading as comparing two funds' fees without checking that they track the same index.

### Sizing discipline: what keeps core-satellite from drifting into something else

Three simple rules keep a core-satellite structure from gradually turning into an undisciplined collection of individual bets. First, the combined satellite allocation is capped at a stated maximum share of the total portfolio, decided in advance rather than in the moment a new idea seems compelling — a common convention is to keep the combined satellite share at 20% or less of the total portfolio, though the appropriate figure depends on an investor's own risk tolerance and time horizon. Second, each individual satellite position is itself capped, so that conviction in a single theme cannot silently become a concentrated single-sector bet larger than the equity core itself. Third, the whole structure is rebalanced back to its target weights on a predetermined schedule or threshold, since a satellite sleeve that performs well will otherwise mechanically grow as a share of the portfolio purely through price appreciation, regardless of whether the original conviction behind it still holds. Part 5 works through the mechanics and evidence on calendar-based versus threshold-based rebalancing in detail; the point to establish here is only that some rebalancing discipline is a necessary companion to core-satellite construction, not an optional refinement to be added later.

### Common ways core construction drifts off course

Three recurring patterns undo otherwise sound core construction, and naming them plainly is cheaper than learning them by experience. The first is performance chasing at the core level itself: reallocating the core away from a broad index and toward whichever narrower fund or sector produced the best trailing return, which quietly converts a diversified core into a concentrated bet dressed up in core-sized weight. The second is home bias taken to an extreme, where the international-allocation decision flagged above is never revisited at all rather than deliberately decided either way — an omission rather than a considered choice, and the more common failure mode in practice. The third is a gradual loosening of the satellite cap discussed earlier: an investor who initially commits to a 15% or 20% satellite ceiling, then makes a series of individually reasonable-sounding exceptions to it when a particular thematic idea seems unusually compelling, ends up with a core-satellite structure in name only. None of these are exotic mistakes requiring sophisticated tools to avoid; they are avoided by writing down the target policy mix and the satellite ceiling before conviction about any specific holding is running high, and revisiting that written policy on a schedule rather than in response to whatever the market did last month.

### A preview of where the accounts fit in

One dimension of core-satellite construction this part has deliberately deferred is which account holds which sleeve — a taxable brokerage account, a traditional employer retirement plan, or a Roth-type account, each introduced briefly in Part 1. The short version, developed fully in Part 4, is that a portfolio's asset location can be optimized alongside its asset allocation: holdings that generate significant ordinary taxable income each year, such as the bond core, are often better placed inside a tax-advantaged account where that income is not taxed annually, while holdings expected to compound with less frequent taxable distributions, such as a broad equity index core, are more tolerable to hold in a taxable account. This does not change any of the weights derived in this part — the target allocation between SPYM, AGG, QQQM, and the satellite sleeves is unaffected by which account each sits in — but it does change how an investor with multiple accounts should distribute the same target weights across them, which is a separate optimization layered on top of, not a substitute for, the allocation decisions made here.

This part has moved from the vocabulary of Part 1 to an actual structure: a broad, low-cost core across at least two asset classes, an optional core-plus tilt built from other broad indices, and a bounded allowance for satellite conviction positions, all held together by sizing rules that prevent any one piece from overwhelming the whole.
