# US Markets and Portfolio Construction
## Part 5 of 5: Implementation, Monitoring, and Long-Term Portfolio Maintenance

The first four parts of this series built a structure and explained the rules governing it: the market landscape and vehicle costs in Part 1, a disciplined core-satellite framework in Part 2, satellite construction and concentration measurement in Part 3, and the risk mathematics, options-income mechanics, and tax rules in Part 4. This closing part addresses what happens after the structure exists — how money actually goes into it over time, how it gets kept in line as markets move, and what changes once an investor starts taking money back out. None of the four earlier parts, on their own, answer the question of timing: Part 2 established target weights, but not when to add new money against them; Part 2 and Part 3 established sizing ceilings, but not how often to check whether those ceilings have been breached; and Part 4 established the tax rules governing a sale, but not when a sale driven by portfolio maintenance is actually worth making. This part closes each of those gaps in turn, then closes the series itself with a single worked example spanning all five parts.

### Dollar-cost averaging versus investing a lump sum immediately

A recurring question for anyone with a lump sum to invest — an inheritance, a bonus, the proceeds of a sale — is whether to invest it all at once or spread it out gradually over several months, a practice commonly called dollar-cost averaging (DCA). Vanguard's own research on this question, examining rolling historical periods across the US, UK, and Australian markets, found that investing a lump sum immediately outperformed spreading the same amount out over 12 months roughly two-thirds of the time [1]. The mechanical reason is straightforward: markets rise more often than they fall over most historical periods, so money that is not yet invested is, on average, sitting out of gains it would otherwise have captured, and every month DCA delays full investment is a month of that opportunity cost. This finding is worth stating precisely rather than loosely, since it is easy to over-read: "two-thirds of the time" also means a full third of historical periods favored gradual entry, and the margin by which lump-sum investing won, in the periods it did win, varied considerably rather than following a single consistent pattern.

### Table 1 — Lump sum versus DCA under two illustrative one-year scenarios

| Scenario | Lump sum ($6,000 upfront) ending value | DCA ($500/month × 12) ending value | Lump sum advantage |
|---|---|---|---|
| Steady rise (illustrative +1%/month) | $6,760.95 | $6,404.66 | +$356.29 |
| Dip in months 1–2, then recovery (illustrative) | $6,618.62 | $6,554.34 | +$64.28 |

Both illustrative scenarios in Table 1 favor the lump sum, consistent with Vanguard's finding — but the gap narrows considerably in the scenario where a decline happens early, since DCA's gradual entry allows some of the later contributions to buy in at the lower, post-decline prices rather than the higher starting price. This is the entire case for DCA in a single sentence: it does not raise expected returns relative to a lump sum, but it reduces the specific regret of investing everything immediately before a decline, and it is the only realistic option in any case for money that arrives gradually, such as a salary, rather than all at once. Vanguard's own conclusion is not that DCA is irrational, but that an investor choosing it is deliberately trading expected return for a smoother emotional path into a full position, a trade psychologically real enough that Vanguard's paper explicitly validates it as a reasonable choice for an investor who would otherwise be tempted to delay investing indefinitely rather than commit a lump sum at all [1].

A middle path worth naming is a compressed DCA schedule — spreading a lump sum over three to six months rather than the full twelve Vanguard's research tested, which captures a meaningful share of the psychological benefit of gradual entry while limiting the expected-return cost, since most of the "opportunity cost of waiting" in the twelve-month case comes from the later months of the schedule rather than the first few. None of this changes the more important practical point for most investors following this series: money arriving from ongoing income, rather than a single windfall, is not actually choosing between DCA and lump-sum investing at all — it is simply being invested as it is received, which is DCA by construction rather than by deliberate choice, and the comparison in Table 1 is only a live decision for a genuine lump sum already sitting in cash.

### Rebalancing in practice: what the research actually supports

Part 2 established that a core-satellite structure needs rebalancing discipline to avoid satellite creep; this part turns to what the evidence says about how to do it. An earlier Vanguard analysis of calendar-based, threshold-based, and combined rebalancing strategies found that, for most broadly diversified stock-and-bond portfolios, risk-adjusted returns did not differ meaningfully across a wide range of specific rules, and recommended annual or semiannual monitoring with a 5% rebalancing threshold as a reasonable, cost-effective default for most individual investors [2]. A more recent Vanguard paper, published in December 2024 and focused on threshold-based methods, found a more specific edge: a threshold-based approach — monitoring an allocation continuously and rebalancing only once it drifts by a set amount, such as 200 basis points, back to a destination somewhat inside that band, such as 175 basis points — outperformed calendar-based rebalancing by roughly 5 to 21 basis points per year on a risk-adjusted basis, with the benefit somewhat larger during retirement withdrawals than during accumulation [3]. Neither finding overturns the other; they agree that some form of systematic rebalancing outperforms no rebalancing at all, and disagree only modestly on how much additional benefit a more sophisticated threshold rule adds over a simple calendar-based one — a genuinely smaller practical question than whether to rebalance at all, and one where an investor is better served by picking either approach and applying it consistently than by spending excessive effort optimizing between two methods the research shows are reasonably close substitutes for most portfolios of the kind this series has built.

### Table 2 — Illustrative one-year drift on the $50,000 core-satellite portfolio (from Part 2)

| Sleeve | Starting weight | Illustrative 1-year return | Ending weight (before rebalancing) | Drift |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| SPYM (equity core) | 55.0% | +15% | 53.5% | −1.53 pp |
| AGG (bond core) | 15.0% | +2% | 12.9% | −2.07 pp |
| QQQM (growth tilt) | 15.0% | +30% | 16.5% | +1.48 pp |
| Satellite blend | 15.0% | +35% | 17.1% | +2.12 pp |

Portfolio value grew from $50,000 to $59,150 under this illustrative set of returns, and the satellite sleeve's weight drifted from its 15.0% target to 17.1% — still inside a 20% combined-satellite ceiling in this particular illustration, but this is precisely the mechanical satellite-creep process Part 2 warned about: a satellite sleeve that simply performs well, with no new conviction added by the investor at all, mechanically grows its share of the portfolio purely through price appreciation. Restoring the original target weights on this $59,150 base requires selling $877.50 from QQQM and $1,252.50 from the satellite blend, while buying $907.50 of SPYM and $1,222.50 of AGG — a disciplined trade that, notably, involves selling some of what just performed best and buying some of what lagged, which is exactly why rebalancing is behaviorally harder to execute than it is mathematically simple to describe.

Rebalancing by selling appreciated positions has a tax consequence in a taxable account that Part 4 already covered in full: any sale of an appreciated position realizes a capital gain, taxed at the rates discussed there, and a rebalancing trade that looks costless on a pre-tax basis can carry a real after-tax cost in an account without tax-advantaged shelter. A widely used way to reduce this friction is to rebalance using new contributions rather than by selling: directing new monthly or periodic contributions preferentially toward whichever sleeve is currently below its target weight, rather than splitting every contribution proportionally across all sleeves regardless of current drift, gradually pulls the portfolio back toward target without generating any taxable sale at all. This approach works well while contributions are large relative to the portfolio's overall size — early in an accumulation period — but becomes mathematically less effective as the portfolio grows large relative to ongoing contributions, at which point some amount of actual selling becomes unavoidable to correct meaningful drift within a reasonable time frame, making contribution-based rebalancing a complement to, rather than a full substitute for, the threshold- or calendar-based selling discussed above.

### A practical monitoring checklist

Bringing together the sizing and rebalancing rules from across this series into a single cadence, a reasonable monitoring routine for an investor running the core-satellite structure built in Parts 2 and 3 checks, at minimum: current sleeve weights against target weights and the rebalancing threshold chosen (Part 2 and this part); the combined satellite share against its ceiling (Part 2); any single satellite position against its own individual cap (Part 2); the effective N of any satellite fund whose underlying index methodology may have changed since it was last checked (Part 3); and, for any options-wheel positions, upcoming expiration dates and the collateral currently committed against them (Part 4). None of this requires daily attention for a portfolio built from the broad, low-turnover vehicles this series has focused on — the Vanguard research cited above specifically found that more frequent monitoring did not meaningfully improve risk-adjusted outcomes for this kind of portfolio — but it does require checking on a fixed schedule rather than only when a market move prompts an emotional reaction to check.

A specific cadence worth naming as a concrete default, consistent with the research cited above: a quarterly check of sleeve weights against the 5% threshold, an annual deeper review of each fund's current expense ratio, structure, and methodology against its issuer's own current disclosures (precisely the kind of check that would have caught the QQQ reclassification and the SOXX stock split discussed earlier in this series before they became a surprise), and a continuous — not periodic — awareness of any open options-wheel collateral commitment, since an assignment can occur at any expiration date rather than on a schedule the investor controls. An investor who prefers a single trigger rather than a calendar date can instead set a portfolio-tracking tool to flag whenever any sleeve drifts past its threshold, which functions as an event-driven version of the same underlying discipline. Whichever cadence is chosen, writing it down in advance — the specific check dates or triggers, and the specific thresholds that prompt action — serves the same purpose the written policy mix served in Part 2: it gives the investor something concrete to follow during a period of market stress, rather than a vague intention to "keep an eye on things" that tends to dissolve into either paralysis or panic exactly when discipline matters most.

### Common mistakes in implementation

Three patterns recur often enough in how portfolios like the one this series has built actually get run, as distinct from how they get planned, to name directly. The first is treating the initial allocation decision as a one-time event rather than a standing policy that needs periodic reconfirmation — an investor's risk tolerance, time horizon, and financial circumstances change over years, and a target allocation set once at the start of a long investing career is not automatically still appropriate a decade later, even if it was well-reasoned when chosen. The second is inconsistent application of the rebalancing rule itself: rebalancing promptly when a decline creates a buying opportunity in a lagging sleeve, but delaying or skipping the equivalent sell-side rebalancing when a satellite has run up sharply, a form of the disposition effect (a well-documented tendency to sell winners too early and hold losers too long, or in this context, to enjoy holding winners and avoid trimming them) that quietly turns a disciplined rule into an inconsistently applied one. The third is abandoning the plan altogether during a sharp decline — exactly the scenario Vanguard's own rebalancing research flagged as a real risk, noting that meaningful deviations during periods of heightened volatility may cause investors to abandon their portfolio strategy altogether — which converts a paper loss that historically would have recovered into a realized, permanent one at the worst possible moment.

### Sequence-of-returns risk and the debate over safe withdrawal rates

Everything so far in this part concerns building up a portfolio; the mathematics changes once an investor begins withdrawing from it, because a withdrawal taken during a market decline permanently removes shares that can no longer participate in the eventual recovery — a phenomenon called sequence-of-returns risk. The point is easiest to see by holding the average return fixed and varying only the order in which it occurs.

### Table 3 — Identical average return, different outcome, purely from timing

Consider two hypothetical retirees, each starting with $1,000,000, each withdrawing a fixed $45,000 per year (a simplified illustration that does not adjust for inflation, in order to isolate the sequencing effect alone), and each experiencing the exact same ten annual returns — eight years, averaging positively, plus two sharp decline years of −20% and −15% — differing only in when the two decline years occur:

| Year | Retiree A (decline years 1–2) | Retiree B (decline years 9–10) |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | $1,000,000 | $1,000,000 |
| 2 | $596,750 | $1,137,500 |
| 5 | $646,952 | $1,249,176 |
| 8 | $653,279 | $1,439,549 |
| 10 | $666,446 | $895,643 |

Both retirees experience an identical average annual return of 3.30% over the ten years and take an identical fixed withdrawal every year — the only difference is whether the two decline years land at the start or the end of the sequence. Retiree A ends with $666,446; Retiree B ends with $895,643, a gap of $229,197 that comes entirely from timing, not from any difference in average performance [4]. This is the central, somewhat unsettling fact behind every "safe withdrawal rate" debate: a withdrawal rate that would have been perfectly sustainable against the average historical return can still fail against the specific sequence a real retiree actually lives through, which is why the research in this area tests every historical starting year individually rather than relying on a single average.

### Where the safe-withdrawal-rate research currently stands

William Bengen's original 1994 research tested this question directly: across every 30-year retirement period in the available historical US data, using a simple stock-and-bond portfolio, the worst-case starting withdrawal rate that never ran the portfolio to zero came out to 4.15%, rounded down to the now-famous 4% figure [5]. The Trinity Study, published a few years later, corroborated a similar figure using a related historical methodology [5]. This is not a settled question, however, and current expert views genuinely diverge: Bengen himself has since revised his own conclusion upward, publishing research in 2025 that raises the historical worst-case figure to 4.7% once a more broadly diversified portfolio across seven asset classes is used instead of the original simple stock-bond mix, and suggesting many retirees could reasonably plan around 5% to 5.5% with some willingness to adjust spending in bad years [6]. Morningstar's own 2026 analysis, by contrast, arrives at a more conservative figure closer to 3.9%, reflecting a more cautious, forward-looking view of starting valuations rather than Bengen's purely historical worst-case framing [7]. This series does not attempt to resolve which figure is correct, since the honest answer is that all of them are, in different senses: Bengen's 4.15%–4.7% range answers "what would have survived the worst historical sequence," while Morningstar's lower figure answers a somewhat different, more forward-looking question about current starting conditions — an investor planning a real retirement is better served by understanding that this range reflects a genuine, ongoing methodological disagreement among careful researchers than by anchoring on any single number as a proven constant, and the disagreement itself is informative: it signals that a withdrawal plan should build in room to adjust rather than assume any single figure is guaranteed to hold for thirty years regardless of how markets actually behave.

Two practical responses to this disagreement are worth naming rather than leaving the debate purely theoretical. The first is a flexible, rather than fixed, withdrawal approach — sometimes called a guardrails strategy — in which the withdrawal rate is adjusted periodically based on portfolio performance: spending is trimmed somewhat after a period of poor returns and allowed to rise somewhat after a period of strong ones, rather than following an unchanging inflation-adjusted dollar figure regardless of what the portfolio has done. This directly addresses the mechanism Table 3 illustrates, since a retiree willing to reduce withdrawals during exactly the kind of early decline Retiree A experienced avoids compounding that decline with an unchanged, and now proportionally larger, fixed withdrawal. The second is a bucket approach, holding one to two years of anticipated spending in cash or short-term instruments, a further few years in the bond core discussed in Part 2, and the remainder in the equity core and satellite structure — explicitly so that a market decline can be waited out using the cash and bond buckets without forcing a sale of equities at depressed prices, which is the specific mechanism sequence-of-returns risk operates through. Both approaches are refinements to, not replacements for, the underlying core-satellite structure built across this series; they change the withdrawal mechanics layered on top of it, not the portfolio construction itself.

### A full worked case study

Bringing every part of this series together, consider an investor implementing the core-satellite structure introduced in Part 2 with a $50,000 starting position, contributing an additional $500 per month thereafter. The allocation follows Part 2's growth-oriented variant: 55% SPYM, 5% AGG, 20% QQQM, 5% SCHD (dividend satellite), and 15% split between SOXQ (sector satellite) and a modest options-wheel allocation sized within Part 4's collateral framework, with each satellite position individually capped and the combined satellite-plus-growth-tilt allocation monitored against a 40% ceiling on non-core holdings. Contributions are invested via DCA by necessity, since they arrive monthly from income rather than as a lump sum, consistent with Part 5's finding that DCA is the natural approach for ongoing contributions even where Vanguard's research favors a lump sum for money already sitting in cash. The portfolio is monitored quarterly against a 5% rebalancing threshold, consistent with the simpler of the two Vanguard rebalancing approaches discussed above, and each satellite position's effective N (Part 3) is rechecked at least annually against the fund's current published holdings, since weighting methodologies and index compositions can and do change, as the SOXX stock split and the QQQ UIT-to-open-end reclassification discussed earlier in this series both demonstrate.

### Table 4 — Ten-year projection of the worked case study (illustrative 8% annual return, before the effect of any rebalancing trades)

| End of year | Projected balance | Cumulative contributions |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | $60,217 | $56,000 |
| 3 | $83,168 | $68,000 |
| 5 | $109,939 | $80,000 |
| 7 | $141,164 | $92,000 |
| 10 | $198,008 | $110,000 |

Over the ten-year illustration, cumulative contributions total $110,000 (the initial $50,000 plus $500 monthly for 120 months) against a projected ending balance of $198,008 — $88,008 attributable to investment growth rather than new contributions, under the clearly illustrative 8% annual return assumption used throughout this table. At the growth-oriented variant's blended expense ratio of 0.095% computed in Part 2, the approximate annual cost of ownership on the year-10 balance alone is around $188 — a small figure in absolute terms at this portfolio size, and a useful illustration of how directly Part 1's cost-layer arithmetic, Part 2's blended-expense-ratio calculation, and this part's accumulation projection all connect to produce a single, checkable number rather than three separate, disconnected exercises. As this investor approaches a spending phase decades later, the withdrawal-rate research summarized above becomes directly relevant, and the sequence-of-returns illustration in Table 3 becomes the reason a bond and cash allocation typically increases as that transition approaches — not because bonds outperform equities over long horizons, but because a portfolio drawing down principal is specifically vulnerable to the timing risk Table 3 demonstrates, in a way a portfolio still purely accumulating is not. A reasonable glide path, consistent with the bucket approach discussed above, would begin shifting this specific growth-oriented allocation toward the more conservative variant introduced in Part 2 over the five to ten years preceding an anticipated withdrawal phase, rather than as an abrupt reallocation on a single retirement date.

### Closing the series

Across five parts, this series has moved from the raw vocabulary of US markets — indices, vehicle types, legal structures, and layered costs — through a disciplined method for combining that vocabulary into a core-satellite structure, a rigorous way of measuring what a satellite position actually concentrates in, the mathematics and tax rules that determine what a portfolio actually keeps, and finally the practical mechanics of running the whole structure over time. The throughline connecting all five parts is less any single number than a method: verify a claim against its primary source before relying on it, whether that claim is about a fund's legal structure, an index's weighting rule, a tax threshold, or a withdrawal rate, because every one of those facts has changed at least once during the research for this series alone and will change again. QQQ's reclassification from a UIT to an open-end fund, SOXX's forward stock split, the annual movement of IRS contribution limits and capital-gains brackets, and the ongoing, genuine disagreement among retirement researchers over safe withdrawal rates are not exceptions to an otherwise stable set of facts about US markets — they are representative of how this entire field actually behaves, and a portfolio-construction method that only works when none of its underlying facts change is not a durable method at all.

What this series has deliberately not done is tell any specific reader what allocation, satellite selection, or withdrawal rate is correct for their own circumstances, since that determination depends on details — risk tolerance, time horizon, tax situation, and personal conviction — that a general series cannot know and should not assume. What it has tried to provide instead is a complete, checkable toolkit: the vocabulary to describe any US-market portfolio precisely, the mathematics to evaluate one quantitatively rather than impressionistically, and the habit of checking every important claim against a source a reader can open and verify themselves, exemplified by the reference list attached to every part of this series. The companion appendices and workbook accompanying this series are built to make that verification habit easy to continue well past the point where this series ends, with every formula, every worked example, and every cited figure laid out so that a reader can substitute their own numbers, their own fund choices, and their own assumptions, and trust the arithmetic that follows because they can see exactly how it was built.
