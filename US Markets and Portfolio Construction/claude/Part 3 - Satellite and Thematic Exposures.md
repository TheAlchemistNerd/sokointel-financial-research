# US Markets and Portfolio Construction
## Part 3 of 5: Satellite and Thematic Exposures — Concentration, the Semiconductor Case Study, and Dividend-Focused Sleeves

Part 2 established the discipline: a bounded satellite allowance sits alongside a dominant core, capped in total size and capped position by position, rebalanced back to target on a schedule. This part opens up what actually goes inside that allowance. A satellite sleeve is, by definition, less diversified than the core it sits beside — that is the entire reason it needs the bounding rules Part 2 introduced — but "less diversified" is not a single amount, and measuring it precisely turns out to matter more than it might first appear. The tools this part introduces — a formal concentration measure, a worked comparison across four funds pursuing the same broad theme through different rules, and a careful distinction between an officially administered index and an informally tracked list — are meant to replace impressions about a satellite's diversification with a specific, checkable number.

### Why investors add a satellite at all

The case for a satellite position is rarely just "this might outperform." More often it reflects a specific, bounded conviction the broad core does not express on its own: a view that a particular sector will benefit disproportionately from an identifiable structural trend, a preference for an income-generating strategy layered on top of existing holdings, or simply a wish to participate more directly in an area of the economy the investor understands well through their own work or research. None of these are illegitimate reasons to hold a satellite position. What Part 2's sizing rules exist to prevent is a satellite conviction, however well-founded, from being sized as though it carried the same diversification the core provides — which it structurally cannot, by the nature of concentrating in a narrower slice of the market.

A closely related, though distinct, motivation is income generation layered on top of an existing position — for example, selling covered calls against a core holding, or running a cash-secured-put strategy to generate premium income while waiting to acquire a position at a target price. Part 4 covers the mechanics, margin economics, and risk profile of one such approach, the options wheel strategy, in full; it is mentioned here only to note that an income-generating options overlay is itself a form of satellite activity subject to the same sizing logic as a sector or thematic fund, even though it does not involve buying a new, separate fund at all. Whatever the specific motivation, the honest test for whether a position belongs in the satellite category rather than the core is not how the investor feels about its prospects, but whether it is built from a broad, representative cross-section of an asset class or from a deliberately narrower slice of it — the same test Part 2 used to disqualify single stocks and narrow sector funds from core status applies without modification to satellite construction, it just no longer disqualifies the position; it only determines how small a share of the portfolio that position should occupy.

### Measuring concentration: the Herfindahl-Hirschman Index

A useful, standard way to quantify how concentrated a fund actually is goes beyond simply counting its number of holdings. The Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI) sums the squared weight of every holding in a fund:

**HHI = Σ wᵢ²**  (Equation 3.1)

where wᵢ is the portfolio weight of holding i, expressed as a decimal. A fund's effective number of holdings — how many equally weighted positions would produce the same concentration — follows directly:

**Effective N = 1 / HHI**  (Equation 3.2)

For a fund holding N positions at exactly equal weight, HHI = N × (1/N)² = 1/N, so effective N equals the nominal holding count exactly — the defining property of an equal-weighted structure. The moment any holding is over-weighted relative to an equal split, HHI rises and effective N falls below the nominal count, even though the fund's fact sheet still reports the same number of holdings. Three illustrative 30-name distributions make the gap concrete:

### Table 1 — Nominal holdings vs. effective diversification

| Weighting structure | Nominal holdings | HHI | Effective N |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pure equal weight | 30 | 0.0333 | 30.0 |
| Illustrative capped modified-cap-weight (top 5 at an 8% cap, next 10 at a 4% cap, remaining 15 sharing the rest) | 30 | 0.0507 | 19.7 |
| Illustrative uncapped, single-name-dominant weighting | 30 | 0.0962 | 10.4 |

It is worth pausing on why the formula squares each weight rather than simply summing them, since the squaring is what gives the measure its meaning rather than an arbitrary mathematical flourish. Summing the raw weights of every holding always produces exactly 1 (or 100%) regardless of how concentrated a fund is, since the weights must add up to the whole portfolio by definition — that calculation carries no information about concentration at all. Squaring each weight before summing gives disproportionately more influence to larger holdings: a 20% position contributes 0.04 to the sum, sixteen times more than a 5% position's contribution of 0.0025, even though the 20% position is only four times larger. This is precisely the property concentration should have — a single dominant holding should move the measure far more than several modest ones — and it is why HHI, originally developed as a measure of market concentration in antitrust economics, translates directly into a measure of concentration within a fund's holdings.

All three rows describe a fund that reports "30 holdings" on its fact sheet, yet the effective diversification ranges from 30 down to roughly 10 depending purely on how the weights are distributed across those holdings — a fact a holdings count alone never reveals, and precisely why weighting methodology, not just holdings count, is the first thing to check before treating any fund as "diversified enough" to satisfy a satellite's sizing rules.

### Turning effective N into a sizing input

Effective N is useful precisely because it converts a methodology difference into a single comparable number that feeds directly back into the position-sizing decisions Part 2 introduced. A satellite sleeve with an effective N near 30 behaves, for diversification purposes, much like the equity core itself scaled down in size — its idiosyncratic, single-company risk is spread widely even though its thematic or sector concentration remains. A satellite sleeve with an effective N near 10, by contrast, carries meaningfully more single-company risk per dollar invested, and an investor sizing two satellite positions at the same portfolio weight without checking this figure could easily end up with very different amounts of single-company risk in each, despite believing the two allocations were sized equivalently. This does not mean a low-effective-N satellite should never be held — a satellite position is permitted to be concentrated, that is the point of calling it a satellite — but it does mean the position-size ceiling appropriate for a low-effective-N fund is reasonably set lower than the ceiling appropriate for a higher-effective-N fund covering the same broad theme, precisely because the former carries more single-company risk per unit of capital deployed.

### Checking effective N in practice

None of the funds discussed in this part publish their Herfindahl-Hirschman Index or effective-N figure directly on their fact sheets, which means calculating it is a short manual exercise an investor can do themselves rather than a number to look up. Every US-listed ETF issuer publishes a full, current holdings list — typically downloadable as a spreadsheet from the fund's own product page — showing each constituent's weight as of the most recent trading day. Squaring each of those published weights and summing the results gives HHI directly from Equation 3.1, and inverting that sum gives effective N from Equation 3.2, using nothing more than the spreadsheet functions already built into any standard spreadsheet program. This is a five-minute check worth performing before sizing any satellite position, and it is considerably more reliable than inferring concentration from a fund's stated holdings count or from a qualitative sense of "how diversified" a sector sounds, given how differently the three illustrative structures in Table 1 can produce the same nominal holdings count.

### A case study in why "semiconductor exposure" is not one thing

Four widely held ETFs give exposure to the same broad theme — US-listed semiconductor and semiconductor-equipment companies — and yet are built on meaningfully different rules, a useful illustration of why the underlying index matters as much as the label on the fund, and why a fund's name can outlive the index it actually tracks. VanEck's SMH, at a 0.35% expense ratio, tracks the MVIS US Listed Semiconductor 25 Index, a modified free-float market-cap-weighted benchmark of roughly 25 names using a banded capping approach that splits constituents into smaller- and larger-weight groups rather than a single flat cap [1]. iShares' SOXX, at a 0.33% expense ratio, no longer tracks the index its name suggests: although SOXX is still widely nicknamed "the PHLX Semiconductor ETF" and its own product-page URL still reads "phlx-semiconductor-etf," BlackRock transitioned the fund's benchmark away from the PHLX Semiconductor Sector Index to the NYSE Semiconductor Index (also referred to as the ICE Semiconductor Index) back on June 21, 2021 [2], [9]. Invesco's SOXQ, priced considerably lower at a 0.19% expense ratio, is the fund that actually tracks the PHLX Semiconductor Sector Index (SOX) today — the 30-name, tiered modified-market-cap-weighted benchmark that gives the sector its well-known "SOX" ticker, which caps its three largest constituents individually at 12%, 10%, and 8% and every other constituent at 4%, redistributing any excess weight proportionally across the remaining names at each quarterly rebalancing [3], [4]. State Street's XSD, at a 0.35% expense ratio, takes a structurally different approach entirely, tracking the S&P Semiconductor Select Industry Index using a true equal-weight methodology across its constituents, rebalanced back to equal weight on a set schedule regardless of any individual company's market capitalization [5].

The practical consequence is that an investor choosing "a semiconductor ETF" is making a real methodology decision, not a purely cosmetic one — and, in SOXX's case, not even the decision its own product name implies. SMH and SOXX, both modified-cap-weighted, will move substantially in line with whichever handful of mega-cap semiconductor names currently dominate the sector by market value, though they answer to two different index committees with two different sets of rules; XSD, by equal-weighting the same broad industry, deliberately gives smaller semiconductor-industry companies the same influence on returns as the largest ones, at the cost of the "effective N" benefit shown in Table 1 above cutting the other way if the largest names happen to outperform. An investor who specifically wants exposure to the PHLX Semiconductor Sector Index — the benchmark that gave the sector its "SOX" nickname in the first place — is better served today by SOXQ than by SOXX, despite SOXX's name and legacy branding, and SOXQ delivers that specific exposure at close to half of SOXX's expense ratio besides.

The word "semiconductor" also masks a real sub-industry split worth naming: the sector includes both chip designers and manufacturers (sometimes further split into "fabless" firms that design chips but outsource manufacturing, and integrated device manufacturers that do both) and semiconductor-equipment makers, whose fortunes depend on capital spending by the chipmakers themselves rather than on chip demand directly. All four funds discussed here draw from both halves of this split under their respective index rules, but the modified-cap-weighted funds (SMH, SOXX, SOXQ) will tilt toward whichever half currently contains the largest companies by market value, while XSD's equal-weight approach gives both halves influence in proportion to their number of constituents rather than their combined market value. An investor with a specific view on, say, equipment spending relative to chip design would need to look inside each fund's actual holdings breakdown rather than assume "a semiconductor ETF" captures that distinction uniformly, and the same caution applies to any thematically labelled fund whose name suggests more internal uniformity than its actual constituent list delivers.

Liquidity is a further point of real difference among the four, tying back to the bid-ask-spread cost layer introduced in Part 1. SMH and SOXX are, by assets and trading volume, considerably larger and more heavily traded than SOXQ and XSD, which typically translates into tighter bid-ask spreads and a smaller premium-or-discount-to-NAV risk for the two larger funds, all else equal. This is a real cost consideration working in the opposite direction from the expense-ratio comparison: SOXQ's lower stated expense ratio can be partly offset, for an investor trading in size or trading frequently, by a wider typical spread than SOXX's — a reminder that Part 1's "total cost of ownership" framing, not the expense ratio in isolation, is the correct basis for comparing satellite vehicles just as much as core ones.

### A live corporate-action example: the SOXX stock split

In August 2026, iShares announced a forward stock split for SOXX, with a record date of November 3, 2026 [2]. A forward split of this kind divides each existing share into a larger number of shares at a proportionally lower price per share; it changes the fund's share count and per-share price, and typically nothing else — the underlying index exposure, the expense ratio, and the investor's proportional ownership of the fund's assets are unaffected. The split is worth naming here for a reason beyond the mechanics themselves: a corporate action of this kind is exactly the sort of detail that can make a fund's own historical share-price chart look discontinuous to an investor checking it casually, and it is a useful reminder that verifying a fund's current structure against its issuer's own current disclosures — rather than relying on a chart or a comparison written before the action took effect — is a standing habit worth carrying through every part of this series, not just the QQQ reclassification discussed in Part 1.

### A satellite is not automatically a diversifier

Appendix 2 showed, in the three-asset variance example built around an illustrative equity core, bond core, and sector satellite, that a satellite sleeve highly correlated with the equity core it sits beside contributes relatively little genuine diversification benefit to the portfolio as a whole, even though it may reduce blended volatility slightly through its smaller weight. A US semiconductor-sector fund is a clear real-world instance of this pattern: it is drawn from the same broad US equity market as a typical S&P 500 or Nasdaq-100 core, and its constituents very often overlap heavily with the largest names already held inside that core through the core's own technology-sector weighting. Adding a semiconductor satellite on top of a Nasdaq-100 core-plus tilt, in particular, is likely to concentrate exposure to a small number of already-heavily-weighted names further, rather than diversify away from them — a reasonable thing to do deliberately, as a sized, bounded conviction position, but a mistake to describe to oneself as "adding diversification" when the honest description is "adding concentration in an area already well represented in the core."

### Dividend-focused satellites: three approaches to the same broad idea

A dividend-focused satellite sleeve pursues a different objective from a sector satellite — steadier income and, often, a quality tilt — but is built through the same kind of rules-based screening logic. Schwab's SCHD, at a 0.06% expense ratio, tracks the Dow Jones U.S. Dividend 100 Index, which requires a minimum ten-year record of consecutive dividend payments and then ranks the eligible universe on a composite of cash-flow-to-total-debt, return on equity, dividend yield, and five-year dividend growth, capping any single holding at 4% and any single sector at 25%, and excluding real estate investment trusts from eligibility entirely [6]. The S&P 500 Dividend Aristocrats Index takes a narrower, longevity-focused approach: eligibility requires membership in the S&P 500 itself, at least 25 consecutive years of dividend increases, a minimum float-adjusted market capitalization of $3 billion, and adequate trading liquidity, with the resulting eligible names equally weighted rather than weighted by any quality or growth composite, and reconstituted annually with quarterly reweighting [7], [8].

A third category — often called "Dividend Kings," referring informally to companies with 50 or more consecutive years of dividend increases — is worth flagging precisely because of the "verifiable, not false positive" standard this series applies throughout. Unlike the Dividend Aristocrats, which is a defined, rules-based index administered and published by S&P Dow Jones Indices with a stated methodology document, "Dividend Kings" has no single official index provider, no one authoritative published methodology, and no single canonical list; it is a category maintained informally across various financial media and community sources, which converge closely but not always exactly on membership because there is no governing rulebook analogous to the Aristocrats' methodology to settle edge cases. An investor building a satellite sleeve around "Kings" companies is, in effect, constructing a self-directed basket of individual stocks rather than buying a single rules-based index product, which changes both the diversification characteristics and the ongoing maintenance burden relative to a fund tracking the Aristocrats index.

### Table 2 — Three dividend-focused approaches compared

| Approach | Governing methodology | Selection criterion | Weighting |
|---|---|---|---|
| SCHD (Dow Jones U.S. Dividend 100 Index) | Published, S&P Dow Jones Indices-administered | 10-year dividend history + quality/growth composite score | Composite-score weighted, 4% single-stock cap |
| S&P 500 Dividend Aristocrats | Published, S&P Dow Jones Indices-administered | S&P 500 member with 25+ consecutive years of dividend increases | Equal-weighted, reweighted quarterly |
| "Dividend Kings" (50+ years) | No single official administrator | Informally tracked 50+ year dividend-increase streak | Not standardized; typically a self-constructed basket |

A dividend-quality satellite of any of these three kinds is also worth sizing with one further caveat in mind: a long dividend-growth streak or a strong quality composite score describes a company's payment history and current financial characteristics, not a guarantee of future performance relative to the broader market, and dividend-focused strategies as a group have gone through extended periods of underperforming a broad, unscreened index, particularly during strongly growth-led market regimes when the companies capable of sustaining and raising dividends for decades tend to cluster in more mature, slower-growing sectors. This is not a reason to avoid a dividend-focused satellite — it is a reason to hold it, like every other satellite discussed in this part, as a deliberately sized conviction position rather than as a substitute for the core, and to expect it to occasionally lag the core for extended stretches without treating that lag as evidence the position was a mistake.

### Sizing a dividend-and-sector satellite sleeve together

Returning to Part 2's worked $50,000 portfolio, the two satellite sleeves in Table 2 of that part — a 7.5% dividend-quality allocation and a 7.5% sector allocation, together summing to 15% of the total portfolio — sit comfortably inside the 15–20% combined satellite ceiling recommended there. Using SCHD for the dividend sleeve and SOXQ for the sector sleeve (chosen over SOXX both for its lower cost and because it is the fund that actually delivers the PHLX Semiconductor Sector Index exposure the "SOX" name implies) keeps the combined satellite expense-ratio contribution modest: at a 0.06% and 0.19% expense ratio respectively, weighted at 7.5% each, the two sleeves together add only about 0.019 percentage points to the portfolio's blended expense ratio, a small fraction of the 0.067% blended figure computed in Part 2. The more binding constraint remains the concentration question this part has focused on: a 7.5% position in a 30-name, modified-cap-weighted sector index with an effective N in the range illustrated in Table 1 is a meaningfully concentrated bet within that 7.5%, even though it is a small, bounded slice of the total portfolio — which is precisely the intended design of core-satellite construction, not a flaw in it.

### Beyond sector and dividend: the wider satellite universe

Sector and dividend-quality sleeves are common starting points, but the same sizing and concentration logic extends to other categories an investor might hold as a satellite rather than a core. A real estate investment trust (REIT) fund, for instance, gives exposure to an asset class with return drivers — rental income, property valuations, interest-rate sensitivity through financing costs — distinct enough from broad equities that it can offer genuine diversification benefit rather than the concentrated-but-correlated pattern a same-market sector fund often exhibits, though REITs carry their own concentration question depending on whether the fund is cap-weighted toward a handful of large trusts or more broadly spread. A gold or broad commodities allocation is another common satellite category, typically added not for its expected return but for its historically low or negative correlation with equities during specific kinds of market stress, functioning more as a portfolio-level hedge than a growth position — a different rationale from the conviction-based sector and dividend sleeves this part has focused on, but subject to the same weight-cap and rebalancing discipline once added. The unifying principle across every category is the one this part has repeated in different forms: know the actual concentration and correlation characteristics of what is being added, rather than assuming a satellite automatically diversifies the portfolio simply because it is a different fund from the core.

### Common ways satellite construction goes wrong

Three failure patterns recur often enough to name directly, echoing the behavioral risks Part 2 flagged for core construction but taking a sharper form at the satellite level, where the bounded sizing is meant to limit the damage of any one going wrong. The first is treating a strong trailing return in a satellite position as license to raise its weight above the pre-committed ceiling — the exact "reasonable-sounding exception" pattern Part 2 warned against, and one that satellite positions are especially prone to precisely because they were chosen for conviction in the first place, making it psychologically easy to justify one more increase. The second is holding multiple satellites that appear diversified on paper — a semiconductor fund, a broader technology fund, and a growth-tilted core-plus position, for instance — without checking how much they actually overlap in underlying constituents, a mistake this part's correlation discussion above was specifically aimed at preventing. The third is neglecting the effective-N and liquidity checks introduced in this part when a new satellite idea is added, treating "it's rules-based and passively managed" as a sufficient diversification credential on its own, when a rules-based fund can still be concentrated by design, as several of the funds discussed in this part demonstrate.

### Where this leaves the series

This part has shown that a satellite sleeve's true diversification depends on its weighting methodology, not just its stated holding count, using the Herfindahl-Hirschman Index to make that gap measurable rather than impressionistic; walked through why four funds tracking "the same" semiconductor theme are meaningfully different products; and distinguished a rules-based, officially administered dividend index from an informally tracked one that carries no such governing methodology. The common thread across all of it is that the word describing a satellite position — "semiconductors," "dividend growth," "diversified sector fund" — is never a substitute for checking the specific rules that translate that word into an actual list of holdings and weights. Part 4 now turns to the mathematics that ties the whole portfolio together — expected return and risk at the total-portfolio level, the mechanics and margin economics of an options-based income strategy, and the tax rules that determine what an investor actually keeps after each of the choices made so far. Appendix 3, attached to this part, extends the HHI calculations shown here, including a worked derivation of the effective-N formula and additional weighting scenarios a reader can adapt to any fund's own disclosed holdings.
