# US Markets and Portfolio Construction
## Part 4 of 5: Risk Mathematics, Options Income, and the Tax Rules That Decide What Investors Keep

Parts 2 and 3 built a structure — a diversified core, bounded core-plus tilts, and sized satellite sleeves — and showed, through worked two- and three-asset examples, how combining assets that do not move in lockstep reduces a portfolio's volatility below what a simple weighted average would suggest. This part first generalizes that arithmetic to a full portfolio of any size, then turns to two subjects that determine what an investor actually keeps after the allocation decisions are made: the mechanics and risk of an options-based income strategy that several of this series' source materials specifically asked about, and the tax rules — on dividends, on realized losses, on capital gains, and on account choice — that apply regardless of which vehicles from Parts 1 through 3 end up in the portfolio. Nothing in this part changes any of the allocation weights or vehicle choices established earlier in the series; it changes how much of what those choices earn actually survives contact with the tax code, and, for the reader specifically interested in an options-income overlay, what that overlay actually is mechanically before any decision is made about whether it belongs in a portfolio at all.

### From two assets to a full portfolio: the general variance formula

Appendix 2's two- and three-asset variance calculations were specific cases of a single general formula that extends to any number of holdings. Collecting the portfolio's weights into a vector w and the pairwise covariances between every pair of holdings into a covariance matrix Σ (where each diagonal entry is a holding's own variance, and each off-diagonal entry is the covariance between two different holdings), portfolio variance in matrix form is:

**σₚ² = wᵀ Σ w**  (Equation 4.1)

Expanded out, this is exactly the double summation shown in Appendix 2's Equation 2.3 — Equation 4.1 is simply the more compact notation standard in portfolio theory and in the spreadsheet and software tools used to compute it in practice once a portfolio holds more than three or four positions, at which point writing out every pairwise term by hand becomes impractical. The core insight carries over unchanged regardless of notation: a portfolio's risk depends on every pairwise relationship between its holdings, not merely on each holding's individual volatility, which is why the core-satellite structure built across Parts 2 and 3 needs to be evaluated as a whole rather than position by position.

A practical caveat belongs alongside the formula rather than left implicit: Σ, the covariance matrix, is not directly observable the way an expense ratio or a stated index methodology is. It must be estimated from historical return data, and that estimate carries real uncertainty — a correlation calculated from the last three years of data can differ meaningfully from one calculated over the last twenty, and neither is a guarantee of the correlation that will actually prevail going forward, a point already illustrated in Appendix 2's sensitivity table showing how much the diversification benefit changes as the correlation assumption alone is varied. This is a limitation of portfolio variance mathematics generally, not a flaw specific to Equation 4.1: the formula is exact given its inputs, but the inputs themselves are estimates of an uncertain future built from an already-uncertain past, which is one reason this series has consistently labelled every volatility and correlation figure used in its worked examples as an illustrative assumption rather than a forecast.

### The options wheel: mechanics of a named income strategy

Several of the source materials behind this series specifically asked about a strategy commonly called the options wheel, a cyclical combination of two standardized, exchange-traded option positions. The wheel begins by selling a cash-secured put (CSP) on a stock the investor is genuinely willing to own at the chosen strike price. Selling a put obligates the seller to buy 100 shares per contract at the strike price if the option is exercised, and a cash-secured put backs that obligation with cash held aside for exactly that purpose — collateral equal to the strike price multiplied by 100 shares per contract, frozen in the account until the position closes [1], [2].

**Worked example — Stage 1, the cash-secured put:** Consider a stock trading at $100, with an investor selling one put contract at a $90 strike for a premium of $2.00 per share. The collateral required is $90 × 100 = $9,000, and the premium collected is $2.00 × 100 = $200. If the stock stays above $90 through expiration, the put expires worthless, the $9,000 collateral is released, and the $200 premium is the investor's full profit — a 2.22% return on the collateral committed for that period, or, if the same trade could be repeated on a similar basis every month with no losses along the way, a simple (non-compounded) annualized figure of roughly 26.7%. That annualized figure is worth treating with real caution rather than as an expected return: it assumes the exact same premium is available every month, that no assignment or adverse price move interrupts the cycle, and it says nothing about the probability of those conditions holding, which depends on option pricing (implied volatility) and market conditions that vary continuously.

One collateral detail is worth stating plainly because it surprises new options traders: the cash-secured put collateral requirement is identical whether the position sits in a cash account or a margin-enabled account, since the "cash-secured" part of the name refers to the full strike-price collateral being set aside regardless of what other margin privileges the account otherwise has [2]. This differs from many other options strategies, where a margin account can reduce the capital required relative to a cash account; the cash-secured put is deliberately structured to require the full purchase amount up front, which is also why its maximum loss is fully defined and bounded rather than open to a margin call for additional funds beyond the collateral already posted.

**Worked example — Stage 2, assignment and the covered call:** If instead the stock falls below $90 and the put is assigned, the investor buys 100 shares at the $90 strike; the $200 premium already collected reduces the effective cost basis to $88.00 per share [3]. The investor then sells a covered call against those shares — for instance, a call at a $95 strike for a $1.50 premium. If the stock rises above $95 by expiration and the shares are called away, the effective sale price is $95 + $1.50 = $96.50 per share, producing a combined gain across both stages of $96.50 − $88.00 = $8.50 per share, or $850 on the 100-share position — a 9.66% return on the effective basis across the full cycle. If the stock instead falls further after assignment — to $70, for instance — the position shows an unrealized loss of $18.00 per share, or $1,800, which the $200 of premium already collected offsets only slightly.

### Table 1 — The wheel's outcomes at each stage

| Stage | Trigger | Outcome | Effective per-share result |
|---|---|---|---|
| CSP expires worthless | Stock stays at/above $90 strike | Collateral released, premium kept | +$2.00 (premium only) |
| CSP assigned | Stock falls below $90 strike | Shares purchased at strike | Cost basis = $88.00 |
| Covered call expires worthless | Stock stays below $95 strike | Shares retained, premium kept | Basis reduced further |
| Covered call assigned | Stock rises above $95 strike | Shares sold at strike | Combined result = +$8.50 vs. original $88.00 basis |
| Adverse scenario | Stock falls sharply after assignment | Unrealized loss on shares held | Premiums collected offset only part of the loss |

### Reading the wheel's risk honestly

The wheel is frequently described as a way to "get paid to wait" for a stock to reach a target entry price, and the mechanics above show why that description is accurate as far as it goes — the premium is real income, collected regardless of outcome, at the moment the option is sold. What that framing tends to understate is that a cash-secured put's maximum loss is not small: because the position obligates the seller to buy shares at the strike price regardless of how far the stock has fallen by expiration, the theoretical maximum loss on the put alone is the strike price minus the premium collected, per share — the same exposure, in economic substance, as owning the shares outright from the strike price down to zero, just arrived at through a different-looking instrument. This is precisely why the strategy is suited only to a stock an investor would genuinely want to own at the strike price, and precisely why, within the core-satellite framework built across Parts 2 and 3, a wheel position on any single stock is satellite activity — concentrated, single-company risk — subject to the same position-sizing ceiling as the semiconductor or dividend satellites discussed in Part 3, regardless of how it is wrapped or how routine the income it generates may come to feel.

A second, less-discussed risk sits on the other side of the trade: opportunity cost when the covered-call leg is assigned in a strongly rising market. Once the covered call is sold, the position's upside is capped at the call's strike price regardless of how far the stock rises beyond it — an investor whose shares are called away at $95 does not participate in any further rally to $110 or $120, since the shares are gone at the point of assignment. This is the mirror image of the downside risk on the put leg: the wheel exchanges unlimited upside participation for defined, collected premium income, on both legs of the cycle, which is a reasonable trade-off for an investor whose objective is steady income rather than maximum capital appreciation, but a poor fit for a position an investor actually expects to appreciate substantially and wants full exposure to.

### Common ways the wheel goes wrong in practice

Three patterns account for most of the difference between the wheel's appeal on paper and its results in practice. The first is selecting strikes based purely on the premium offered rather than on genuine willingness to own the stock at that strike — chasing a higher premium by selling closer to the current price increases assignment probability and, with it, the chance of being assigned during exactly the kind of sharp decline the worked downside example above illustrates. The second is running the strategy on a single name or a small handful of related names for the sake of premium yield, which reintroduces the concentration risk Part 3 spent considerable space quantifying, now wrapped in an options structure that can make the underlying concentration less visually obvious than a simple stock position would. The third is treating the strategy's realized premium income as if it were the position's total return, when the correct comparison — as the downside worked example shows — nets that income against any unrealized loss on shares still held; a wheel position that has collected several months of premium while its underlying shares have fallen well below the effective cost basis is not a profitable position merely because the premium income is real and already banked.

### How the wheel is taxed

The tax treatment of both legs is specific and, in places, counter-intuitive relative to how the income feels day to day. If a sold put or covered call simply expires worthless, IRS Publication 550 treats the premium collected as a short-term capital gain, recognized on the expiration date, regardless of how long the option position itself was open [4]. Because it is a short-term gain by rule, that premium income is taxed at ordinary income rates rather than the more favorable long-term rates discussed later in this part, and, for higher-income investors, may also be subject to the 3.8% Net Investment Income Tax that applies to investment income above statutory thresholds — a further reason the wheel's stated income yield is not directly comparable to a qualified dividend yield of the same headline size. If a covered call is instead assigned, the premium is not taxed separately at all; it is folded into the proceeds of the stock sale, and the holding period governing long-term versus short-term treatment is the holding period of the underlying stock, not the option [4], [5]. If a cash-secured put is assigned, the premium is not taxed immediately either — it reduces the cost basis of the newly acquired shares, exactly as shown in the worked example above, deferring any tax consequence until those shares are eventually sold [5]. One further wrinkle is worth flagging without fully resolving here: selling a call that is deep in-the-money or very short-dated relative to its strike can, under the IRS's "qualified covered call" rules, suspend the holding period of the underlying stock, converting what would have been a long-term gain into a short-term one — a detail significant enough that an investor writing calls against a long-held position should confirm the specific call they intend to sell qualifies before assuming their stock's holding period is unaffected.

### Qualified versus ordinary dividends

Separately from options income, the dividends generated by the core and satellite holdings discussed throughout this series are not all taxed identically. A dividend qualifies for long-term capital-gains tax rates, rather than ordinary income tax rates, only if the underlying shares are held for more than 60 days during the 121-day period beginning 60 days before the stock's ex-dividend date [6] — a rule aimed specifically at preventing an investor from buying a stock briefly, collecting a dividend, and claiming preferential tax treatment for what is otherwise a very short-term transaction. Because it is a holding-period test, satisfying it does not require any special election or additional paperwork; broker-reported 1099-DIV forms already classify each dividend as qualified or not based on the holding-period facts the broker has on record. It is also worth connecting this rule back to Part 3's discussion of dividend-focused satellites: distributions from real estate investment trusts are generally not qualified dividends at all, regardless of holding period, because REIT distributions are typically treated as ordinary income for tax purposes under the distinct rules governing REIT taxation — one of several reasons SCHD's methodology, discussed in Part 3, excludes REITs from its eligible universe entirely, keeping its distributions on more consistent qualified-dividend footing than a fund that included them would have.

### Table 2 — Qualified vs. non-qualified dividend, illustrative $1,000 payout

| Classification | Applicable rate (illustrative) | Tax owed | Net received |
|---|---|---|---|
| Qualified dividend | 15% (long-term capital-gains rate) | $150.00 | $850.00 |
| Non-qualified (ordinary) dividend | 24% (illustrative ordinary marginal rate) | $240.00 | $760.00 |

The $90 gap on a single $1,000 payout illustrates why the 61-day holding-period test is worth checking deliberately rather than assumed, particularly for an investor whose trading pattern around ex-dividend dates is more active than a typical buy-and-hold core position — the core, core-plus, and satellite vehicles discussed throughout this series are generally held long enough that this is not a live concern, but any position bought and sold in a shorter window around a dividend date should have its qualification status checked rather than assumed.

### The wash-sale rule and tax-loss harvesting

A tax-loss harvesting strategy — deliberately selling a position at a loss to realize that loss for tax purposes — runs directly into the wash-sale rule: if an investor sells a security at a loss and buys the same or a substantially identical security within 30 days before or after the sale, the loss is disallowed for current-year tax purposes and instead added to the cost basis of the newly acquired position [7]. Brokers are required to track and report wash sales on covered securities directly on Form 1099-B, adjusting the reported basis automatically when a wash sale is detected within a single account — though this automated tracking generally does not extend across an investor's different accounts at different brokers, or between a taxable account and an IRA, which means an investor repurchasing a "substantially identical" position in a different account entirely can trigger a wash sale the broker-generated tax forms will not catch on their own, leaving the investor responsible for tracking and reporting it correctly. Consider selling 100 shares bought at $50 for $40, realizing a $1,000 loss, and then repurchasing shares at $41 within the 30-day window: the $1,000 loss is disallowed this year, and the new position's basis becomes $41 × 100 + $1,000 = $5,100, or $51.00 per share, rather than the $41.00 per share actually paid. The loss is not destroyed — it is deferred into a higher cost basis on the replacement shares, reducing whatever gain (or increasing whatever loss) is eventually realized when those replacement shares are sold — but an investor expecting to claim the loss in the year of the original sale will be surprised to find it disallowed if the repurchase happened inside the window. The wash-sale rule is also worth connecting back to the options strategies discussed earlier in this part: selling a put option on a stock shortly after selling that same stock at a loss can itself trigger the wash-sale rule, since an option to acquire a security can count as acquiring a "substantially identical" position depending on the specific terms of the contract — a subtlety worth raising with a tax professional before combining tax-loss harvesting with an options-income strategy on the same underlying name, rather than assuming the two activities are automatically independent of each other.

### 2026 long-term capital gains brackets

For assets held more than a year, the 2026 long-term capital-gains brackets for a single filer tax gains at 0% up to $49,450 of total taxable income, 15% on the portion between $49,450 and $545,500, and 20% above that, under Rev. Proc. 2025-32 [8]. Because these are marginal brackets applied to total taxable income (ordinary income plus the gain, stacked), the effective rate on a given capital gain depends on the taxpayer's other income as much as on the gain itself:

### Table 3 — Effective long-term capital-gains rate at different income levels (single filer)

| Ordinary taxable income | Long-term capital gain | Tax owed | Effective rate on the gain |
|---|---|---|---|
| $40,000 | $20,000 | $1,583 | 7.9% |
| $100,000 | $50,000 | $7,500 | 15.0% |
| $600,000 | $100,000 | $20,000 | 20.0% |

A gain that is fully inside the 0% bracket when a taxpayer's other income is low can be taxed at up to 20% for an otherwise identical gain realized by a higher-income taxpayer — a genuine planning consideration for the timing of a large realized gain, though one this series flags rather than resolves, since it depends heavily on an individual's full tax picture rather than the portfolio-construction questions this series otherwise addresses. A related, practical implication of Table 3 is that the choice of which specific tax lot to sell, when a position was built up through multiple purchases at different prices and different times, can itself change which bracket a given sale's gain falls into — most brokers allow an investor to specify particular lots at the time of sale rather than defaulting to a first-in-first-out ordering, a choice worth making deliberately for a position with meaningfully different purchase prices across its history rather than left to the broker's default. One further scoping note applies throughout this section: every figure above is a federal tax figure. State-level taxation of dividends, capital gains, and options income varies enormously across the United States — several states levy no tax on any of this income at all, while others tax it at rates that meaningfully change the after-tax comparisons shown in Tables 2 and 3 — and an investor applying this section's arithmetic to their own situation needs to layer their specific state's treatment on top of the federal figures shown here, which this series does not attempt to catalogue given how significantly it varies by jurisdiction and changes over time.

### Asset location, formalized

Part 2 previewed the idea that a target allocation and the account holding each piece of it are two separate decisions; this part closes that loop. A taxable brokerage account exposes every dividend, every short-term capital gain from options premium, and every unqualified distribution to tax in the year received, while a traditional 401(k) or IRA defers tax until withdrawal and a Roth-type account can eliminate it on qualified withdrawals entirely [9]. Holdings that generate a steady stream of ordinary-rate taxable income every year — a bond core, or an options-income strategy whose premiums are taxed as short-term gains regardless of holding period — are, all else equal, better placed inside a tax-advantaged account, where that annual tax drag disappears; a broad equity core that generates mostly qualified dividends and unrealized appreciation is comparatively more tolerable to hold in a taxable account, since qualified dividends already receive favorable rates and unrealized gains are not taxed until sold at all.

### Table 4 — A general asset-location starting point by sleeve

| Sleeve (from Parts 2–3) | Typical tax character | Better suited to |
|---|---|---|
| Bond core (BND/AGG) | Ordinary-rate interest income, taxed annually | Tax-advantaged account |
| Options-wheel satellite | Short-term gains on premium, taxed regardless of holding period | Tax-advantaged account, where the account type permits options trading |
| S&P 500 / total-market core | Mostly qualified dividends plus unrealized appreciation | Taxable account is more tolerable |
| Dividend-quality satellite (e.g., SCHD) | Mostly qualified dividends | Taxable account is more tolerable |
| Sector satellite with high turnover expectations | Potential short-term gains if actively traded | Tax-advantaged account, if trading is frequent |

This table is a starting point rather than a fixed rule, since an individual's specific account balances, employer plan options, and whether a given account even permits options trading at all will constrain how fully it can be implemented in practice — a Roth IRA, for instance, is often cited as an ideal home for an options-income strategy specifically because every dollar of premium, assignment gain, or qualified dividend inside it can potentially be withdrawn tax-free in retirement, but only if the account provider permits options trading within it and only within whatever contribution limits and income restrictions apply to that account type. For 2026, the annual contribution ceiling for a 401(k), 403(b), or similar employer plan is $24,500 for an employee under 50, and the combined traditional-and-Roth IRA contribution limit is $7,500, both set by the Internal Revenue Service [10] — ceilings that constrain how much of a target allocation can actually be sheltered in a given year, regardless of how well-optimized the theoretical asset-location plan looks on paper. An investor with more taxable assets than tax-advantaged space to shelter them in will necessarily hold some less tax-efficient sleeves in a taxable account simply for lack of room, which is a real-world constraint the table above does not resolve on its own.
