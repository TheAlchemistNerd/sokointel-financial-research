# YouTube presentation production plan

## Canonical public links

Use the canonical article page in every YouTube description, card, end screen and pinned comment: `https://sokointel.com/read/<article-slug>/`. Use `https://sokointel.com/watch/` for the episode library and `https://sokointel.com/library/` for the complete reading library. The project storyboards list each article's exact URL.

The programme now covers eleven projects and forty-one article episodes: the original twenty-five articles across seven series, plus sixteen articles across the four Owner Earnings and startup-funding projects. The original thirteen-episode storyboard package remains as source material. Each article receives its own PowerPoint, linked through the presentation master index. Each episode starts with a recognisable household or business decision, shows the research questions, develops an explanation using visible evidence, and returns to those questions with clear answers. The episode plans specify the content and evidence needed for the future decks. They are production plans; PowerPoint decks and videos are the next production stage.

## Audience and voice

Speak as a thoughtful guide exploring a decision with the viewer. Use concrete phrases such as “Let’s follow the cash”, “Here is what changes when the rate moves”, and “We can compare these choices using the same starting amount”. Explain a technical term at the moment it becomes useful. Keep assumptions close to the relevant calculation, with the period, currency and unit visible. Give the audience the information needed to evaluate the trade-off and a useful next step. Research supports the conversation; it should never become an argument with a reader or an earlier draft.

An educational framing can be stated once in the opening or description: “We’ll use worked examples to explore the choices and the reasoning behind them.” Thereafter, show the inputs and explain what the result means. Identify an assumed rate as an assumption, a company observation by its financial year, and a rule by its jurisdiction and date. This supplies useful context without filling the presentation with precautionary language.

## Visual system

Use a 16:9 canvas with a 1920 by 1080 video target. Use Sokointel’s warm ivory, deep forest green, sage and restrained terracotta accent. Keep the existing visual evidence in its original form; use the publication brand for slide framing, headings and annotations. Maintain one main visual or argument per slide. Use generous whitespace, direct labels and a consistent reading order. A feature-rich episode should contain varied evidence and well-designed explanations while each individual slide stays calm.

Use 40–48 point titles and 24–30 point body text as design targets. Charts and tables should remain readable in a phone-sized preview; target 20–24 point labels and keep essential evidence out of tiny footnotes. Put short source attribution and the observation date on the slide, with the full URL and IEEE reference in speaker notes and the description. Use no more than two typefaces, with a standard font such as Aptos or Arial for dependable editing. A supporting calculation may use Cambria Math.

The layout library should contain a minimal opening slide, a research-question slide, a full-width chart, a compact comparison table, a cash-flow diagram, a worked-example slide, a scenario comparison, and an answer slide. Keep evidence charts, tables and requested diagrams editable in PowerPoint. Rebuild the analytical content from source cells or data files; existing PNGs provide reference designs and recording assets. Use photography only when it helps explain a real location, object or activity. Avoid decorative imagery behind figures and avoid dashboard-like grids of small panels.

## Charts and calculations

Every chart specification must name the source, units, period, transformations, scenario assumptions, and intended conclusion. A bar chart normally starts at zero. A line chart may use a tighter scale when it is labelled clearly and serves the comparison. Keep percentage changes separate from percentage-point changes. Use consistent scales for comparable scenarios. Show nominal and real values in separately labelled views. Label simulation percentiles as conditional outcomes, with the path count and experiment assumptions available in the notes.

Use an animated reveal to explain a calculation in this order: inputs, relationship, substitution, result and interpretation. The downloadable deck must still show the full completed calculation. Put lengthy derivations, the formula dictionary and the full source table in appendix slides. Speaker notes should include pronunciation, units, arithmetic and the link to the corresponding workbook cells. A spreadsheet recording should zoom into one small range with row and column headings visible, then return to a clean result slide.

## Episode and recording structure

Plan approximately 18–24 minutes for a core episode, then adjust the runtime after reading the narration aloud. The storyboard timings are production estimates. Give a calculation enough time to follow, and split a dense section into an additional slide instead of reducing the type size. Each episode includes its own case assumptions and explanation of the terms it uses. Viewers can watch any episode without having watched the preceding one.

The episode opening identifies the decision and the questions to answer. The middle develops the mechanism and the evidence, then tests an alternative. The closing gives a direct answer to each selected research question and shows a practical exercise the viewer can complete. A later episode can invite the viewer to explore the next topic without making the current explanation depend on it.

Record narration in a conversational pace, leaving short pauses after a number or chart transition. Add accurate captions and a transcript. Use fades and progressive reveals only where they guide attention. Keep a static final state for every animated slide, avoid background music during dense calculations, and keep spoken source attribution brief. Assemble YouTube chapters around questions and decisions. Each description should include the episode questions, research date, article and workbook links, source register and chapter timestamps from the final edit.

## Production sequence

1. Lock the episode question map and audience case. Read the article and appendix together, and record the date for each rule or company observation used.
2. Prepare an evidence table containing the claim, source URL, observed period, workbook range, transformation and planned slide. Update time-sensitive transaction status and policy observations for the intended recording date.
3. Build the data charts and comparison tables as editable objects. Reconcile the plotted series and displayed calculations with the model. Keep assumed inputs explicitly labelled.
4. Write the slide copy and a full spoken script. The visible slide must convey the point independently; narration adds explanation and context. Put complex derivations in the deck appendix.
5. Apply the visual system, then review every slide at full size and in a phone-sized preview. Resolve crowded tables, weak contrast and inconsistent labels.
6. Read the episode aloud, adjust slide changes to the spoken explanation, and record the narration and selected workbook demonstrations.
7. Produce the video, captions, transcript, thumbnail and description. Add chapter timestamps after the edit is stable.
8. Export the editable PPTX, PDF handout and final video. Check that every research question has an explicit answer slide and that the downloadable material contains complete charts, labels and sources.

## Handoff for each future episode

The production folder should contain the editable deck, a PDF handout, narration script, transcript, captions, source and data ledger, thumbnail, video master and publishing description. Track each deliverable separately in the presentation master index. The current production milestone adds editable episode decks and evidence captures to the storyboards and reading editions. Narration recordings, final videos, captions and publication remain separate production steps; a deck does not establish that a video has been recorded or uploaded.
