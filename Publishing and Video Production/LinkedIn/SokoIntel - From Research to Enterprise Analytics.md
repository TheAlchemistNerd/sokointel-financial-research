# SokoIntel: From Financial Research to Enterprise Analytics

<!-- Editorial status: Draft for review. Prepared 25 September 2026. Not posted externally. Body begins below this comment and ends before the Sources heading. -->

A business owner reads an article about cash flow and recognises the problem immediately: sales have grown, yet paying suppliers still feels like a weekly negotiation. An investor reads about valuation and understands the formula, but struggles to reconcile it with a company's accounts. A finance team inherits a spreadsheet whose final number looks plausible, although nobody can explain every adjustment. These are the situations shaping SokoIntel: moments when understanding needs to become a calculation, and a calculation needs to support a decision.

The direction I am building towards is financial research that readers can inspect, apply and eventually use within professional workflows. Two established businesses help explain different parts of that ambition. The Motley Fool offers a useful comparison for research, distribution and membership. Moody's Analytics offers a useful comparison for the institutional destination: analytical tools connected to the work people perform repeatedly. These are reference points for the product strategy, not claims of affiliation, endorsement or equivalent capabilities.

The Motley Fool's service catalogue combines research, stock recommendations, portfolio features and analytical tools across memberships. The relevant lesson for SokoIntel is the continuing relationship around decisions. An article can introduce a problem; useful tools and continuing coverage give someone reasons to return as their circumstances change. That relationship is more demanding than attracting attention once. It requires relevance, clarity and enough practical value for a reader to keep using the service. [The Motley Fool service catalogue](https://www.fool.com/services/)

Moody's Lending Suite illustrates a different relationship. Its described capabilities connect loan structuring and pricing, credit assessment, underwriting and ongoing monitoring. Information developed during a credit decision can support later oversight. The comparison concerns this continuity between analysis and work. Moody's also distinguishes its ratings division from its other data, analytics and decision solutions. SokoIntel's planned analytical workbench belongs in the latter comparison; building financial models does not establish a credit ratings franchise. [Moody's Lending Suite and business distinction](https://www.moodys.com/web/en/us/solutions/lending.html)

For SokoIntel, the progression is research, public calculators, deeper workbooks, professional tools and institutional services. Each stage must earn its place. The publishing platform is central to that progression: it is where people discover the ideas and reveal which questions deserve better tools. The marketplace and membership model can test whether the work is useful enough to pay for. These channels should inform the enterprise product's development while continuing to serve readers who only need education or an occasional calculation.

There is already substantial material to build from. The research programme includes articles, technical appendices, editable workbooks and video presentation assets. The web platform has calculators and publishing infrastructure. The next challenge is to make the connections between those assets dependable. A reader should be able to move from an explanation to its worked example, change an assumption, understand the result and find the evidence behind it. That continuity matters more than adding another disconnected feature.

Owner earnings provides a particularly useful thread through the programme. Buffett's original discussion asks what a business earns after allowing for the investment needed to maintain its competitive position and operating capacity, including additional working capital where required. Estimating that investment involves judgement. SokoIntel's research uses that judgement as a subject to examine openly, with assumptions the reader can challenge. A single confident number can conceal exactly the uncertainty that matters to an owner or investor. [Berkshire Hathaway's 1986 shareholder letter](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/1986.html)

Project 1 applies the question to small businesses and MSMEs. An owner needs to distinguish reported profit, sustainable earnings and the amount that can prudently leave the business. A profitable workshop may still need cash for machine replacement, tax obligations, loan repayments and a difficult trading month. A distributor may need to finance customers before receiving payment. The practical product opportunity is a connected operating toolkit: a cash forecast, working capital schedules, maintenance planning and an explicit assessment of owner withdrawals.

That work connects directly to Making Business Credit Work and the research on key person risk. The business owner asks what financing will help achieve. The lender asks where repayment will come from and when. Both need reconciled records, a clear financing purpose and a view of adverse conditions. The same evidence can support those conversations, although their decisions differ. A growing order book, for example, means little to a repayment schedule until collection dates, delivery costs and existing obligations are understood.

Project 2 carries the analysis into listed companies, including US businesses and companies on the Nairobi Securities Exchange. Its articles and workbook already explore filing reconstruction and different business profiles. The detailed public calculator suite is now being developed. The intended tools will let users examine the bridge from reported earnings to owner earnings, distinguish maintenance from growth investment, assess dilution, explore valuation ranges and reverse the calculation to examine assumptions implied by a price.

Sector differences are essential to that work. A semiconductor designer, data centre operator, agricultural producer and commercial bank require different questions and adjustments. For banks and insurers, the research must examine capital and the capacity to absorb losses or claims. For other issuers, replacement spending, leases, network investment or working capital may dominate the analysis. The planned calculators should make those distinctions visible, with separate methods where necessary, rather than present one result as comparable across every company.

The current Project 2 workbook provides a starting point through its NVIDIA earnings bridge, asset investment examples, simplified bank illustration and NSE evidence matrix. Those examples do not yet constitute complete valuation models for every company covered. The next release should preserve that distinction: supported filing inputs, analyst assumptions and teaching scenarios need separate treatment. Expanding the interface should never make the evidence appear more complete than it is.

The data work is equally important. SEC APIs provide access to filing histories and structured financial statement information, making them a useful foundation for US company analysis. However, retrieving a figure does not settle its interpretation. Our analytical process must preserve the issuer, period, currency, units, filing date and source, then reconcile the figure with the relevant accounts and notes. For NSE cases, the corresponding evidence will come from issuer reports and relevant market and regulatory sources. [SEC EDGAR API documentation](https://www.sec.gov/search-filings/edgar-application-programming-interfaces)

This is also why archived research code needs careful assessment before becoming a public product. A prototype can contain valuable ideas while depending on shortcuts that are unsuitable for a published calculator. Each reused function needs a clear financial definition, representative examples and independent checks. A reader changing a maintenance assumption should understand what changes elsewhere in the model. Missing inputs should remain visible instead of quietly becoming zeros that create a persuasive valuation.

Project 3 turns towards failure retrospectives and warning systems. Its research combines owner earnings with working capital, funding runway, governance and supplier and customer behaviour. The aim is to reconstruct what could reasonably have been observed at a particular date. This requires care with hindsight: a later failure does not prove that every earlier decision was obviously wrong. Where company records are incomplete, an illustrative scenario must be labelled as a scenario, with the gaps left explicit.

The companion work on startup funding figures changes the unit of analysis again. Here the subject is a market and its funding cohorts. A national total should be examined alongside deal counts, concentration, financing type and comparable periods. A company's announced fundraising requires a separate investigation into what was actually provided and on what terms. These are research questions, not automatic verdicts. The programme will distinguish amounts raised from losses and dated operating outcomes from broad labels about success or failure.

Together, these projects suggest three connected views: the business, its financing contract and, where relevant, a vehicle holding receivables. The business view examines sustainable cash generation. The financing view examines obligations on their payment dates. The receivables view examines the assets, collections, reserves and payment priorities supporting investors. Developing these views together could help SokoIntel connect owner decisions, lender analysis and more advanced financial structures without confusing their different claims on cash.

The immediate work should remain practical. Public calculators need clear inputs, visible formulas, defined symbols and useful explanations of the results. A table should tell the reader whether an amount is annual or monthly, before or after tax, reported or assumed. An example should reconcile. The workbook should then deepen the same question with schedules, scenarios and evidence. When an article, calculator and workbook disagree, the discrepancy should be investigated and corrected before more layers are added.

For example, consider an analyst estimating sustainable cash from a listed manufacturer. The article would explain why reported depreciation may differ from replacement needs. The calculator would expose the maintenance assumption and show its effect on the estimate. The workbook would carry the supporting asset schedules and alternative scenarios. A future professional workbench would preserve the reviewer's adjustments and the evidence available at the decision date. That is a concrete workflow to develop and test.

The enterprise architecture is planned around a shared C# financial core, Excel-DNA, selective VSTO integration, and Azure services using the latest supported ASP.NET and .NET versions at implementation. The existing Django publishing and marketplace system remains part of the product. This preserves the investment already made in distribution while allowing professional calculation and institutional services to develop with their own requirements. The purpose of the architecture is consistency of definitions and results across the places where users work.

Institutional use would introduce further obligations. A team needs to know who changed an assumption, which model produced a result, who reviewed it and which evidence was available. Access must reflect responsibilities, and confidential information must remain controlled. Reproducible reports, version histories, review processes and organizational identity integration are therefore part of the intended workbench. They are development requirements, not a claim that today's public website already supplies an enterprise analytical environment.

A further design goal is continuity when information changes. A new filing should create an identifiable update, with the previous analysis still understandable in its original context. A corrected formula should carry an explanation of affected outputs. This would let users distinguish changes in a business from changes in the model, strengthening both the educational material and the professional record of decisions.

The same discipline should shape how we use feedback. Someone can reveal that a calculator is confusing without sharing a confidential portfolio. An accountant can explain a recurring reconciliation problem without uploading client records. We should learn from observed problems, consented feedback and carefully scoped demonstrations. Distribution becomes valuable when it helps identify specific work people need to complete, while respecting the information they hold and the limits of what they have agreed to share.

Consumer demand and institutional demand also require different evidence. A reader buying a workbook demonstrates a form of willingness to pay. An institution adopting a model asks additional questions about validation, reliability, support and integration. Page views and downloads can identify interest, but they cannot establish that a credit team will incorporate the product into its decisions. Paid pilots, repeated professional use and measurable improvements in work would provide stronger evidence for the enterprise direction. Those are milestones to pursue, not achievements being claimed.

That distinction should influence product sequencing. We can first test whether people understand and repeatedly use a public tool. We can then improve its workbook counterpart with clearer inputs, reconciliations and scenarios. A professional pilot should concentrate on a bounded problem with a reviewable result, such as rebuilding an earnings bridge or comparing financing schedules. Only after that should we broaden the workflow. Useful depth will help us discover which capabilities deserve sustained investment.

My intended position for SokoIntel is therefore clear: financial research connected to transparent, reusable decision tools for investors, business owners and finance teams, beginning with Kenyan and African use cases. The Motley Fool comparison helps explain the relationship with readers and members. The Moody's Analytics comparison helps explain the ambition for professional work. The connection between them must be built through useful calculations, traceable evidence and demonstrated demand. That is the work now in front of us.
